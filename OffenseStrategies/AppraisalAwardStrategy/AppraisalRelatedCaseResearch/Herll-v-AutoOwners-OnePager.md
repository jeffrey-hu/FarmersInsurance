# Case One-Pager: *Herll v. Auto-Owners Ins. Co.*

**Citation:** *Herll v. Auto-Owners Ins. Co.*, 879 F.3d 293 (8th Cir. 2018), *vacating and remanding* the underlying judgment of the U.S. District Court for the District of Minnesota. Applies Minnesota insurance law (diversity).

**Why it is in this file:** The facts mirror our matter almost exactly — a hailstorm damaged the windows of an insured Minnesota home, the insurer and homeowners disagreed on the amount of loss, and the dispute went to the policy's appraisal process. The case is the controlling Eighth Circuit authority on what happens when an appraisal award is unclear.

## Facts and Posture

A hailstorm damaged windows on the Herlls' Minnesota home. The Herlls and Auto-Owners could not agree on the amount of the loss, so — as their policy required — they submitted the loss to an appraisal panel. The panel issued a written award, but the award was internally unclear: it referred back to "see above" without identifying which of several preceding dollar figures it meant, and the parties disagreed over whether it covered all of the windows at issue. The Herlls read the award to require the larger payment; Auto-Owners read it to require the smaller. The district court resolved the ambiguity itself on summary judgment, adopting the homeowners' (larger) reading. Auto-Owners appealed.

## Holding

The Eighth Circuit vacated the summary judgment and remanded with directions to **return the award to the appraisal panel for clarification**. The award was "reasonably susceptible of more than one interpretation, and [was] therefore ambiguous." A reviewing court, the court held, "is prohibited from ignoring the ambiguity and summarily affirming the award." When an appraisal award is ambiguous, the court does not weigh the merits and pick an interpretation; it sends the award back to the appraisers — the parties' bargained-for fact-finders — to say what they meant.

## Legal Implications for Appraisal

The practical takeaways for an appraisal-based dispute in Minnesota:

First, **ambiguous appraisal awards are not for the court to construe.** If the award is open to more than one reasonable reading, the correct outcome is remand to the panel, not a judicial guess — even where one party has already been granted summary judgment. This protects the policyholder's bargained-for right to have the appraisers, not a judge, fix the amount of loss, but it also means an unclear award produces delay rather than finality.

Second, **the appraisal award must do the work — clarity at the panel stage is decisive.** *Herll* shows that a vague award (a "see above," an unlabeled alternative figure, an unstated finding on matching) reopens the dispute and can cost a policyholder a year or more. The lesson, especially for the insured, is to make sure the panel's award states plainly which items/areas are covered, the exact dollar figures, and any findings on contested questions such as whether reasonably matching replacement materials are available.

Third, *Herll* sits inside Minnesota's broad view of what appraisers decide. Under *Quade v. Secura Ins.*, 814 N.W.2d 703 (Minn. 2012), "amount of loss" already includes **causation** (e.g., whether hail caused the damage), and under *Cedar Bluff Townhome Condo. Ass'n v. American Family*, 857 N.W.2d 290 (Minn. 2014), it includes the cost of **matching** undamaged materials. *Herll* adds the procedural rule that governs whatever the panel produces: if its resolution of those questions is ambiguous, the answer is clarification by the panel, not interpretation by the court.

## Application to This Matter

Because our claim is the same fact pattern — hail, windows, a Minnesota home, a coverage/amount dispute with the insurer — *Herll* is directly useful in two ways. It supports routing the amount-of-loss dispute (including causation) to appraisal, consistent with Minnesota's preference for the process. And it is a warning to control the form of any appraisal award: insist the panel state, in unambiguous terms, exactly which windows are covered, the precise repair/replacement figures, and any finding on matching, so the award is enforceable rather than a second round of litigation.

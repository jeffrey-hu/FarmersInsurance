# Case One-Pager: *Mork v. Eureka-Security Fire & Marine Ins. Co.*

**Citation:** *Mork v. Eureka-Security Fire & Marine Ins. Co.*, 230 Minn. 382, 42 N.W.2d 33 (Minn. 1950) (Minnesota Supreme Court). Applies Minnesota insurance law.

**Why it is in this file:** *Mork* is the foundational Minnesota authority dividing the labor between appraisers and courts. It establishes that an appraisal panel fixes the *amount of loss* (a fact question) while *coverage* is a question of law reserved to the courts — and that an appraisal award is presumptively valid. Both points bear directly on how Farmers can and cannot use the appraisal in our hail/window dispute.

## Facts and Posture

St. Paul homeowners suffered property damage from freezing. Under the policy's statutory appraisal/arbitration clause, a board of appraisers fixed the loss at $1,137.25. The parties stipulated that the owners had actually spent $1,877.27 repairing the damage — so the award came to roughly 60.5% of actual repair cost. The appraisers also stated that the loss was "not covered" by the policy. The homeowners challenged the award.

## Holding

Two holdings matter here.

First, **an appraisal/arbitration award is presumptively valid.** It "is attended with every presumption of validity" and will not be vacated unless it clearly appears to be the product of fraud, misfeasance, malfeasance, or other wrongdoing by the appraisers. A reviewing court will not disturb the amount merely because it looks low next to the actual cost of repairs.

Second, **appraisers cannot decide coverage.** The appraisers' finding that the loss was "not covered" was outside their province — coverage is a question of *law* for the courts, not the appraisal panel. That portion of the award was "mere surplusage" and was neither final nor binding.

## Legal Implications for Appraisal

The practical takeaways for an appraisal-based dispute in Minnesota:

First, **the appraiser/court divide is firm: amount of loss vs. coverage.** Appraisers value the loss; courts decide whether the policy responds and how it should be construed. A panel that purports to rule on coverage exceeds its authority, and that ruling has no binding effect.

Second, **appraisal awards are hard to overturn.** Absent fraud or misconduct, the award carries a strong presumption of validity — a sword and a shield. It protects a favorable award, but it also means an unfavorable award on *amount* is difficult to attack, so the time to get the numbers right is at the panel stage.

Third, *Mork* sits at the root of Minnesota's modern appraisal doctrine. Later cases refined it without displacing it: under *Quade v. Secura Ins.*, 814 N.W.2d 703 (Minn. 2012), "amount of loss" includes **causation** (e.g., whether hail caused the damage), so appraisers may decide causation when necessary to value the loss — but, consistent with *Mork*, they still cannot make the legal determination that the loss is *not covered*.

## Application to This Matter

*Mork* is directly useful in two ways. It means Farmers **cannot** use the appraisal panel — or any appraiser statement — as a binding ruling that the window damage is "not covered." Coverage is a legal question for the court; any such statement embedded in an appraisal result is surplusage. If Farmers attempts to force the coverage question into appraisal, or to dress a coverage denial up as an appraisal outcome, *Mork* is the answer. Conversely, if Farmers concedes coverage but disputes how much of the window damage the 7/13/2024 storm caused, *Mork* (via *Quade*) confirms that causation-as-amount belongs to the appraisers. And because awards are presumptively valid, the family should weigh carefully before challenging any unfavorable award on amount — the bar to vacate it is high.

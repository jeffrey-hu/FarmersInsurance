# Significance Assessment — The Garage Window Prieve Never Inspected

**Question presented (Bill, 5/30/2026).** On cross-check of Prieve's engineering report (File #241008) against the actual building, the family found no photograph or narrative mention of the **2-sash window on the rear of the garage, behind the shed.** That means Prieve did not inspect all of the windows during his 10/9/2024 site visit. How significant is that omission for the upcoming lawsuit?

**Scope of this memo.** This is a focused significance assessment that builds on the existing §6B treatment in `EngineerReportRebuttal.md` and the damage catalog in `../AppraisalAwardStrategy/01_DamageCatalog_PrieveAppendixA.md`. It is strategic analysis, not legal advice.

---

## 1. Bottom line up front

The missed window is **a real and useful point, but its value is leverage, not damages.** Rated honestly:

- **As a standalone money claim: low.** Nobody has yet documented hail damage *to* that window. A window Prieve skipped is not, by itself, a covered loss the court can value. Its dollar value is whatever damage a proper inspection later shows — currently unknown.
- **As an impeachment / credibility lever against Prieve and Farmers: high.** It converts an abstract "the report is biased" argument into a concrete, binary, admit-or-deny fact: *there is a window in your scope that you never looked at.* That is the kind of fact a jury understands in one sentence.
- **As a foundational-reliability (expert-exclusion) ground: moderate-to-high,** because it goes to whether Prieve's "no recent hail damage" opinion rests on a complete enough factual base to be admissible at all.
- **As a reserved-coverage anchor under *Quade*: high and clean,** because a window no one inspected is necessarily a window the appraisal panel never evaluated — so coverage and amount for it are reserved to the court no matter what Farmers argues about the appraisal.

**The single most important caveat:** as of today this is still **the family's account, not documented evidence.** Until it is pinned down with a dated photograph and a measured site sketch (see §6 below and the ⚠️ flag in `EngineerReportRebuttal.md` §6B), it cannot safely go into a filing. The significance ratings above all assume that documentation gets created. Do that first.

---

## 2. Why a *missed* window is different from the report's other defects

The eight defect categories in `EngineerReportRebuttal.md` (scope limitation, omitted observations, self-contradictions, 2020-file contradictions, public-record errors, methodology gaps, biased framing, reliance gaps) all attack how Prieve **handled damage he actually saw.** Farmers can answer each with "that is a difference of professional opinion."

A window Prieve **never observed at all** is categorically different and harder to spin:

- It is not an interpretation dispute. It is a completeness defect — a gap in the factual record the report claims to cover.
- It is inside Prieve's own stated scope. His engagement was to "evaluate the condition of windows in the home as relates to hail." A windows-only evaluation that omits a window is, on its own terms, incomplete. He cannot say "that was outside my scope" — windows *were* the scope.
- It is provable with a tape measure and a camera, not with competing expert opinions.

That is why this point punches above its dollar weight: it is the cleanest factual concession the family can force out of Prieve.

## 3. Significance channel by channel

### 3.1 Breach of contract (Count I) — a reserved, un-appraised coverage question

Under **_Quade v. Secura Insurance_, 814 N.W.2d 703 (Minn. 2012)**, an appraisal panel determines the "amount of loss," which *includes* causation, but **coverage questions remain reserved to the court.** The corollary that matters here: the panel's binding effect extends only to items the panel actually appraised. A window that **no one ever inspected** — not Prieve, and therefore not the panel that worked from Prieve's record — could not have been appraised. Coverage and amount of loss for that window are necessarily reserved to the court under Count I.

This window is the **cleanest possible example** of the reserved-coverage theory the family is already running for the ~8 other documented-but-unawarded windows (`01_DamageCatalog_PrieveAppendixA.md` §F). For those windows Farmers can at least argue the panel implicitly considered them. For *this* window there is no such argument — it appears nowhere in the 217-photo appendix, so there is nothing to suggest anyone evaluated it. It belongs in the damage catalog as its own line item, separate from the rear/east garage window at A-213–217 (a different window, south of it on the same rear elevation).

### 3.2 Statutory bad faith (Minn. Stat. § 604.18) — the investigation prong

Minnesota's first-party bad-faith statute, as construed in **_Peterson v. Western National Mutual Insurance Co._, 946 N.W.2d 903 (Minn. 2020)**, has two prongs:

1. **Objective:** whether a reasonable insurer, *after conducting a full investigation and a fair evaluation that considers and weighs all of the facts*, would still have denied the claim; and
2. **Subjective:** whether the insurer knew, or recklessly disregarded / remained indifferent to information that would have told it, that it lacked a reasonable basis to deny.

The missed window feeds **prong 1 directly.** Farmers' denial rests on Prieve's report. If the report is built on an inspection that skipped a window in its own scope, then the denial did not follow a "full investigation" of "all of the facts" — it followed an admittedly partial one. That undercuts the "fairly debatable / reasonable basis" defense that Farmers will lead with.

It also supports **prong 2** circumstantially: an insurer that adopts and relies on an engineer's "no damage to the windows" conclusion without the engineer having looked at all the windows is at least reckless as to whether the conclusion has a reasonable basis.

**The § 72A.201 connection — read this carefully.** Minn. Stat. § 72A.201 (Unfair Claims Practices) expressly prohibits "denying a claim without having made a reasonable investigation." But **§ 72A.201 has no private right of action** — an insured cannot sue on it directly; enforcement runs through the Minnesota Department of Commerce (which is why the family's 11/19 Commerce complaint exists). So the correct use of the missed window is **not** "Farmers violated 72A.201, therefore we win." It is: the 72A.201 reasonable-investigation standard supplies the *benchmark of reasonableness* that the § 604.18 bad-faith analysis borrows. The missed window is evidence that Farmers fell below that benchmark, which supports the 604.18 claim. Frame it that way and it is strong; frame it as a standalone 72A.201 cause of action and it gets dismissed.

### 3.3 Expert reliability — and an important correction: this is **Frye-Mack**, not Daubert

`EngineerReportRebuttal.md` §6B and the Prieve deposition plan repeatedly call the expert-exclusion motion a **"Daubert"** motion. In the chosen forum that label is wrong, and it matters.

**The case is filed in Hennepin County District Court — Minnesota state court** (per `KeyDecisions.md`). Minnesota has **not** adopted Daubert. It applies the **Frye-Mack** standard together with the foundational-reliability requirement of **Minn. R. Evid. 702** (see _Goeb v. Tharaldson_, 615 N.W.2d 800 (Minn. 2000)). Two practical consequences:

- **The vehicle is a Frye-Mack / Rule 702 foundational-reliability challenge,** not a Daubert motion. Every "Daubert motion" reference in the strategy documents should be re-labeled "Frye-Mack / Rule 702 motion" for the state-court filing. (Keep a Daubert version in reserve: if Farmers removes to federal court — possible given the Fire Insurance Exchange diversity question in `KeyDecisions.md` — Daubert would then govern, so the existing analysis is not wasted.)
- **Frye-Mack is, if anything, *more* favorable here.** Under Frye-Mack the "general acceptance" requirement can apply to the expert's *conclusions*, not just the methodology — and the foundational-reliability prong requires the proponent to show "that the test itself is reliable *and that its administration in the particular instance conformed to the procedure necessary to ensure reliability.*"

The missed window targets that second clause precisely. Prieve's operative method is visual inspection of each window for hail signatures. If he did not inspect every window, then his administration of his own method "in the particular instance" did not conform to what reliability requires. An expert who reaches a property-wide "no recent hail damage" conclusion without examining all of the property's windows has applied his method to an **incomplete set of facts** — a recognized foundational-reliability defect under Rule 702. This stacks on top of the methodology gaps already cataloged (no impact-force analysis, no terminal-velocity calc, no peer-reviewed support for the "smooth/rounded vs. scrape-mark" diagnostic in §6A).

### 3.4 Corroborative / narrative value — the "what else did he skip?" inference

The window's location is the point. It is roughly 2'×2', on the rear of the garage, south of the documented rear/east window, partially blocked by a shed, reachable only through a narrow gap or by passing through the shed. The natural inference — once the obstruction is documented — is that **Prieve inspected what was easy to see and skipped what took effort to reach.** That inference is corrosive to the report as a whole: if he skipped an obstructed ground-level window, the reliability of his "no hail dents" findings at the *less* accessible locations (the upper-story windows he says he reached "via a ladder") is fairly open to question. It lets the family argue selective inspection across the whole property from a single, concrete anchor.

## 4. Honest assessment of the limits — what Farmers will say back

A balanced memo has to state the other side. Farmers' likely responses, and how they land:

1. **"No damage has been shown on that window, so nothing is owed for it."** Largely correct *today*, and it caps the standalone damages value at zero until the family inspects the window and finds (or doesn't find) hail damage. This is the biggest limit. The point's value is leverage and reliability, not a guaranteed line-item recovery — unless a later inspection shows damage, in which case it also becomes an uncovered, un-appraised loss.
2. **"A small fixed window blocked by a shed was reasonably inaccessible."** A plausible reasonableness defense to the bad-faith framing — but it cuts both ways. If the window was genuinely inaccessible, a careful engineer notes the limitation in the report ("one garage window could not be accessed due to an obstruction"). Prieve's report contains *no such limitation note*; it simply omits the window. The absence of a disclosed limitation is itself the impeachment: either he didn't know the window existed (incomplete identification) or he chose not to reach it and didn't disclose that choice (incomplete, undisclosed scope).
3. **"It was outside the dwelling scope / a detached-structure question."** Watch this one. If the garage is a *detached* structure it may fall under a different policy coverage (Coverage B / other structures) than the dwelling windows, which could complicate the "within his scope" argument. Confirm from the policy and the plat whether the garage is attached or detached before leaning on the "windows were the whole scope" line. If attached, the argument is clean.
4. **"Harmless — one small window doesn't change the conclusion about the rest."** This is why the point should be deployed as *corroboration and reliability*, not as the centerpiece. Standing alone a single missed window is survivable for Farmers; layered onto the downspout/flashing omissions (§2 of the rebuttal), the self-contradictions (§3), and the self-defining diagnostic (§6A), it is one more brick showing the inspection and report were results-driven.

**Net:** treat it as a **force multiplier on the existing reliability and bad-faith arguments**, and as the cleanest *Quade* reservation example — not as an independent winner.

## 5. Where it changes the existing strategy

- **Pleadings.** Add it to the Count I reserved-coverage allegations as a discrete unevaluated window, and to the Count (bad faith) "incomplete investigation" allegations. Do **not** plead a freestanding § 72A.201 violation (no private right of action); plead it as evidence supporting § 604.18.
- **Relabel the motion.** Change "Daubert" to "Frye-Mack / Minn. R. Evid. 702" throughout the state-court strategy docs (`EngineerReportRebuttal.md` §§6A.4, 6B.2; `Deposition/2.1Prieve_Nathan.md`; the `Motions/Daubert/` folder). Keep a federal-removal contingency copy under the Daubert framing.
- **Discovery.** The §6B.4 and §2.5 requests already cover this: subpoena the **full camera download** from 10/9/2024 (not just the curated Appendix A), Prieve's field notes, and any inspection worksheet. The target fact: confirm there is **no** photograph of the rear-of-garage window anywhere in his file.
- **Deposition.** Add a short, tight sequence to `Deposition/2.1Prieve_Nathan.md`: (1) confirm the scope was all windows on the home; (2) confirm the appendix documents exactly one garage window, on the rear/east elevation; (3) establish he did not photograph or note any other window on the rear of the garage (south of the documented one); (4) establish whether he knew it existed; (5) establish his standard practice for obstructed windows and whether he disclosed any access limitation in this report (he didn't). Keep it factual and binary — do not argue.

## 6. The one thing to do before this is usable

Per the ⚠️ verification flag already in `EngineerReportRebuttal.md` §6B, the point is **not filing-ready** until the family creates the proof:

1. **A dated photograph** of the rear-of-garage window showing the shed obstruction and the window's relationship to the documented rear/east window (A-213–217) and house wall.
2. **A measured site sketch** locating the window relative to the documented rear/east window, the shed, and the house wall, with approximate dimensions.
3. **A note confirming the access path** (the gap between shed and house wall, or through-the-shed access).
4. **A policy/plat check** on whether the garage is attached or detached (affects the scope argument — see §4.3).
5. *(If feasible and safe)* a look at the window itself for hail signatures, since finding damage there would upgrade the point from "incomplete inspection" to "incomplete inspection that missed actual covered damage."

Once those exist, the missed window moves from "the family's account" to documented evidence and can be carried into the complaint, the discovery requests, and the Prieve deposition.

## 7. One-paragraph version (for the family / counsel)

Prieve was hired to evaluate all the home's windows for hail damage, and his own 217-photo appendix documents exactly one garage window — on the rear/east side. The 2-sash window on the *rear* of the garage, behind the shed, appears nowhere in his report, which means he never inspected it. That omission is worth real strategic weight: it is the cleanest example of a window the appraisal panel could not have evaluated (a coverage question reserved to the court under *Quade*); it undercuts Farmers' "we did a full investigation" defense to bad faith under Minn. Stat. § 604.18; and it supports excluding or discounting Prieve's "no recent hail damage" opinion under Minnesota's **Frye-Mack / Rule 702** reliability standard (not "Daubert" — this is state court), because an expert who didn't look at all the windows applied his method to an incomplete factual record. Its limits are that no damage to that specific window has been documented yet (so it is leverage, not a guaranteed dollar recovery) and that Farmers will argue the shed made it reasonably inaccessible — which is exactly why the family needs a dated photo and a measured sketch of the window and its obstruction before this goes into any filing.

# Case One-Pager: The *Frye-Mack* Standard

**Citations:** *Frye v. United States*, 293 F. 1013 (D.C. Cir. 1923); *State v. Mack*, 292 N.W.2d 764 (Minn. 1980); reaffirmed in *Goeb v. Tharaldson*, 615 N.W.2d 800 (Minn. 2000). This is the **controlling Minnesota standard** for the admissibility of novel scientific evidence. Applies in Hennepin County District Court.

**Why it is in this file:** *Frye-Mack* — not *Daubert* — is the standard our expert-exclusion motion actually runs on. The "Daubert motion" in this folder is named for the federal case, but because our matter is in Minnesota state court, the governing framework is *Frye-Mack* plus Minn. R. Evid. 702. This one-pager explains the two-part *Frye-Mack* test, how it differs from *Daubert*, and how it shapes the attack on Nathan Prieve's report. Read alongside `Daubert-v-MerrellDow-OnePager.md` (the federal counterpart Minnesota rejected).

## Origins and Posture

*Frye-Mack* is a composite of two cases. **`Frye`** (1923, D.C. Circuit) excluded an early lie-detector test, holding that scientific evidence is admissible only once the technique has gained **"general acceptance"** in the relevant scientific field — the famous "general acceptance" test that governed expert evidence in most American courts for decades. **`Mack`** (1980, Minnesota Supreme Court) adopted and extended *Frye* in Minnesota: the court barred testimony derived from a pretrial **hypnotic** interview because hypnotically-induced "memory" was not shown to be reliable, applying *Frye*'s general-acceptance logic to scientific reliability. Minnesota courts thereafter fused the two into the **"Frye-Mack standard."** In 2000, *Goeb v. Tharaldson* — a toxic-tort case over pesticide exposure — squarely considered whether to switch to the federal *Daubert* approach and **declined**, retaining *Frye-Mack*.

## The Standard

*Frye-Mack* is a **two-prong test** that gates *novel* scientific evidence:

1. **General acceptance (the *Frye* prong).** The underlying scientific technique or principle must be generally accepted as reliable in the relevant scientific community. The court defers to the scientists, rather than independently weighing the science itself.

2. **Foundational reliability (the *Mack* prong).** The particular evidence must have foundational reliability — i.e., the technique must have been correctly applied in the case at hand, with proper procedures and controls, so the specific result is trustworthy.

Both prongs must be satisfied. The first asks whether the *method* is accepted in the abstract; the second asks whether it was *done right* here.

## How It Differs From *Daubert*

This contrast is the practical reason the standard matters to us:

- **Who decides reliability.** Under *Frye-Mack*, the court **defers to the scientific community** on whether a method is sound (general acceptance). Under *Daubert*, the trial judge is an active **"gatekeeper"** who independently assesses reliability using factors like testability, error rate, and peer review.
- **Why Minnesota kept *Frye-Mack*.** *Goeb* reasoned that the general-acceptance test promotes uniformity and objectivity and does not force judges — who usually lack scientific training — to resolve cutting-edge scientific disputes themselves. The court saw deference to the field as more reliable than ad hoc judicial gatekeeping.
- **Scope.** *Frye-Mack*'s general-acceptance prong applies to **novel** scientific evidence. Established, non-novel methods are not subject to the *Frye* hearing, though they still must satisfy Rule 702.

## Relationship to Rule 702

*Frye-Mack* does not stand alone; it operates **inside Minn. R. Evid. 702.** As construed in *Doe 76C v. Archdiocese of St. Paul & Minneapolis*, 817 N.W.2d 150 (Minn. 2012), Rule 702 requires that expert testimony be (a) offered by a **qualified** witness, (b) **helpful** to the jury, (c) supported by **foundational reliability**, and (d) — where the evidence is novel and scientific — compliant with **`Frye-Mack`**. So a Rule 702 challenge can proceed on the foundational-reliability prong **without** ever invoking *Frye-Mack* if the opinion is not "novel science." That distinction governs how we frame the Prieve motion.

## Application to This Matter

The motion against Prieve is best built on **Rule 702 foundational reliability**, and the *Frye-Mack* general-acceptance prong is largely a side issue here — for a deliberate reason. Prieve's visual hail-damage inspection is not "novel science"; it is ordinary forensic-engineering observation, so it likely clears the *Frye* general-acceptance bar and we should not stake the motion on arguing otherwise. The real defect is the **second prong / foundational reliability**: Prieve's causation opinions rest on no quantitative methodology — no terminal-velocity calculation, no material-specific dent-depth modeling, no peer-reviewed support — and his "no hail after 2020" assertion is a weather-records question with no scientific foundation at all. That is a foundational-reliability failure under Rule 702 as informed by the *Mack* prong, not a general-acceptance fight.

Two tactical points follow. First, **cite the Minnesota line as controlling** — *Goeb*, *Mack*, *Doe 76C* — and use *Daubert*/*Joiner*/*Kumho* only as persuasive support; leading with *Daubert* invites Farmers to point out, correctly, that Minnesota rejected it. Second, if Farmers tries to attack **our** expert's HailShapes-based methodology under *Frye-Mack*, the answer is that the peer-reviewed hail-physics framework is **well-established, not novel**, so the general-acceptance prong is satisfied and the methodology is foundationally reliable as applied. The standard cuts in the family's favor on both sides of the expert dispute.

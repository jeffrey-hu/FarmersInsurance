# Case One-Pager: *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*

**Citation:** *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993) (U.S. Supreme Court). Construes Federal Rule of Evidence 702. Federal law; **not binding on Minnesota state courts.**

**Why it is in this file:** This is the case the "Daubert motion" in this folder is named for. The motion package seeks to limit Nathan Prieve's expert testimony, and "Daubert motion" is the common shorthand for a challenge to the admissibility of expert opinion. But the label is partly a misnomer for our matter: the case is in Hennepin County District Court, and **Minnesota has expressly declined to adopt the *Daubert* standard.** This one-pager explains what *Daubert* actually held, and — just as importantly — why our motion runs on Minnesota's *Frye-Mack* / Rule 702 framework instead.

## Facts and Posture

Two minor children and their parents sued Merrell Dow, alleging that the morning-sickness drug Bendectin caused the children's birth defects. The plaintiffs' case depended on expert testimony — re-analysis of epidemiological data and animal studies — concluding the drug was a teratogen. The trial court excluded the plaintiffs' experts and granted summary judgment for Merrell Dow, applying the old *Frye* "general acceptance" test and finding the plaintiffs' methods were not generally accepted. The Ninth Circuit affirmed. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to decide the standard for admitting expert scientific testimony in federal court.

## Holding

The Supreme Court vacated and remanded. Two points control.

First, **the *Frye* "general acceptance" test is superseded by Federal Rule of Evidence 702.** The 1975 enactment of the Federal Rules displaced *Frye*'s rigid common-law test; general acceptance is no longer a precondition to admissibility in federal court.

Second, **the trial judge is a "gatekeeper"** who must ensure expert testimony is both relevant and reliable. The Court offered a flexible, non-exclusive list of factors for assessing reliability of scientific evidence: (1) whether the theory or technique can be and has been **tested**; (2) whether it has been subject to **peer review and publication**; (3) the **known or potential error rate** and the existence of standards controlling its operation; and (4) whether it has attracted **general acceptance** in the relevant scientific community (now one factor among several, not the whole test). The inquiry focuses on methodology and principles, not conclusions.

The companion cases complete the "*Daubert* trilogy": *General Electric Co. v. Joiner*, 522 U.S. 136 (1997) (abuse-of-discretion review; a court may exclude an opinion where the "analytical gap" between data and conclusion is too great), and *Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael*, 526 U.S. 137 (1999) (the gatekeeping duty applies to *all* expert testimony — technical and experience-based, not just "scientific").

## Legal Implications for Expert Testimony

First, **federal and Minnesota state courts apply different standards.** This is the key practical point. In 2000, the Minnesota Supreme Court in *Goeb v. Tharaldson*, 615 N.W.2d 800 (Minn. 2000), **expressly declined to adopt *Daubert*** and retained the *Frye-Mack* standard for novel scientific evidence. Because our case is in Hennepin County District Court, *Daubert* itself is persuasive background only — not controlling authority. Citing *Daubert* to a Minnesota state judge as if it governs would be a mistake.

Second, **Minnesota's actual framework is *Frye-Mack* plus Rule 702.** *Frye-Mack* gates *novel* scientific evidence and asks two things: whether the underlying technique is generally accepted in the relevant scientific community, and whether it has foundational reliability as applied. Separately, Minn. R. Evid. 702 — as construed in *Doe 76C v. Archdiocese of St. Paul & Minneapolis*, 817 N.W.2d 150 (Minn. 2012) — requires that expert testimony be (a) given by a qualified witness, (b) helpful to the jury, (c) supported by **foundational reliability**, and (d) where applicable, satisfy *Frye-Mack*. The reliability concepts overlap with *Daubert*'s, but the legal hook is different.

Third, **the shared idea that survives the state/federal split is foundational reliability of the *specific* opinion.** Both regimes refuse to let an expert's general qualifications carry an opinion that lacks methodological support. Under either standard, an opinion reached with *no* discernible methodology — no testing, no calculation, no peer-reviewed basis — is vulnerable. That is the conceptual core our motion exploits, even though the controlling label is Rule 702 / *Frye-Mack*, not *Daubert*.

## Application to This Matter

The motion in this folder is correctly built on **Minn. R. Evid. 702 foundational reliability**, not on *Daubert* — and the README reflects that. *Daubert* is useful to us in three limited ways. It supplies the persuasive vocabulary (testability, error rate, peer review) that a Minnesota judge will recognize when we attack the gaps in Prieve's report — no terminal-velocity calculation, no material-specific dent-depth modeling, no peer-reviewed support for his causation opinions. It frames the "analytical gap" point from *Joiner*: a court may exclude an opinion where the leap from data to conclusion is unsupported, which is precisely the defect in Prieve's window-by-window causation conclusions. And *Kumho* answers any argument that Prieve's "experience-based" engineering judgment is exempt from reliability scrutiny — it is not.

The one caution: when we file, we style the motion as a **motion in limine under Rule 702 / *Frye-Mack***, and we cite *Goeb*, *Doe 76C*, and the Minnesota line — using *Daubert*, *Joiner*, and *Kumho* only as persuasive support. Leading with *Daubert* as controlling law would invite Farmers to point out, correctly, that Minnesota rejected it.

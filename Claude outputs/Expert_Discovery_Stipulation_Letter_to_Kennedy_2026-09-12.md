Counsel:

We write to propose a short stipulation on expert discovery, and we raise it now — roughly six months before it matters — because by the time it matters it will be too late to fix.

The proposal is mutual. It gives Defendant the same thing it gives Plaintiffs, and on the current schedule Defendant arguably needs it more. A draft is enclosed as Attachment A.

---

**1. The problem**

Three provisions combine in a way we do not think either party intends.

**First, in Minnesota an expert deposition is not available as of right.** Minn. R. Civ. P. 26.02(e)(1)(A) permits a party to identify the other side's trial experts by interrogatory. Anything beyond that falls under Rule 26.02(e)(1)(B): "Upon motion, the court may order further discovery by other means, subject to such restrictions as to scope and such provisions … as the court may deem appropriate." Deposing a testifying expert therefore requires a motion, a hearing, and an order setting scope — not simply a notice of deposition, as it would in federal court.

**Second, the Scheduling Order closes discovery before either expert is disclosed.** Paragraph 2 provides that "[a]ll discovery must be completed by March 15, 2027." Paragraph 3 sets Plaintiffs' expert disclosures and reports at March 15, 2027, Defendant's at April 17, 2027, and rebuttal disclosures at May 14, 2027. Defendant's expert is therefore named a month after all discovery has closed, and the rebuttal expert two months after.

**Third, there is no room left to bring the motion.** Paragraph 5 requires that all dispositive and non-dispositive motions be *heard* by May 14, 2027. Under Minn. Gen. R. Prac. 115.04, a non-dispositive motion must be served and filed 21 days before the hearing — which means a motion for leave to depose Defendant's expert, disclosed April 17, 2027, would have to be filed by roughly April 23, heard by May 14, and the deposition completed within that same window. Hennepin County hearing dates are reserved well in advance. We do not think that sequence is achievable by either party, and we would rather not find that out in April.

**A related point on paragraph 3(a).** That paragraph sweeps Plaintiffs' "expert interrogatories, disclosures, and reports" into the March 15, 2027 date. Read one way, that would bar Plaintiffs from serving the Rule 26.02(e)(1)(A) expert interrogatory on Defendant at all, since Defendant does not designate until April 17, 2027. We assume that is not the intended reading, and the enclosed stipulation confirms it rather than leaving it to be argued later.

---

**2. Why a stipulation is the right instrument**

Minn. R. Civ. P. 29 provides:

> "Unless otherwise directed by the court the parties may by stipulation (1) provide that depositions may be taken before any person, at any time or place, upon any notice, and in any manner, and when so taken may be used like other depositions, and (2) modify other procedures governing or limitations placed upon discovery, except that stipulations extending the time provided in Rules 33, 34, and 36 for responses to discovery may, if they would interfere with any time set for completion of discovery, for hearing of a motion, or for trial, be made only with the approval of the court."

Clause (1) reaches the timing of the depositions. Clause (2) reaches the motion requirement itself, which is a "limitation[] placed upon discovery." The exception is confined to stipulations extending response time under Rules 33, 34 and 36, and does not apply here.

**We nonetheless propose submitting the stipulation to the Court for entry as an order**, for two reasons. The dates in question appear in a scheduling order, and Minn. Gen. R. Prac. 111.04 provides that such an order "may be amended at a pretrial conference or upon motion for good cause shown" and that, "[e]xcept in unusual circumstances, a motion to extend deadlines under a scheduling order shall be made before the expiration of the deadline." A stipulation the parties present jointly, months before the deadline, is the cleanest showing of good cause available and satisfies the timing requirement comfortably. An entered order also removes any argument in April 2027 that the agreement was informal.

---

**3. What we propose**

The enclosed draft is short, and we are not attached to its particular wording. Its four operative terms are:

1. **No motion required.** Each party may depose any expert the other discloses under paragraph 3 as a testifying expert, without the motion that Rule 26.02(e)(1)(B) would otherwise require.

2. **A window that actually exists.** Expert depositions may be taken after the March 15, 2027 general discovery deadline, with all expert discovery completed by **July 16, 2027** — after the June 1, 2027 mediation deadline and a month before the August 16, 2027 pretrial conference. That date disturbs no deadline the Court has set. It is the same date we proposed in our letter of September 11, 2026.

3. **Expert interrogatories.** Either party may serve a Rule 26.02(e)(1)(A) interrogatory directed to experts the other party discloses, within 21 days after that disclosure, with responses due 30 days later — so that paragraph 3(a) is not read to foreclose the one form of expert discovery the rules give as of right.

4. **Fees.** Each party bears its own retained expert's fee for time spent testifying at deposition. Rule 26.02(e)(3) otherwise applies. We are equally willing to adopt the Rule 26.02(e)(3) default in full if Defendant prefers it; we propose the simpler allocation only because it avoids a fee dispute between the parties in the middle of a deposition window.

The stipulation expressly preserves the March 15, 2027 fact-discovery deadline and every other date in the Scheduling Order, and waives no objection either party may have to any expert's qualifications, methodology, or testimony.

---

**4. Why we think Defendant should want this**

Plaintiffs' expert disclosures and report are due **first**, on March 15, 2027. Defendant will hold Plaintiffs' report for a full month before its own is due. On the literal reading of paragraph 2, however, Defendant could not depose Plaintiffs' expert either — that deposition would also fall after the close of all discovery, and would also require a motion under Rule 26.02(e)(1)(B) heard by May 14, 2027.

Whatever the parties' disagreements elsewhere, neither side benefits from reaching trial having been unable to depose the other's expert, and neither benefits from litigating that question on an emergency basis next spring. The symmetry is the point: this stipulation gives each party exactly what it gives the other.

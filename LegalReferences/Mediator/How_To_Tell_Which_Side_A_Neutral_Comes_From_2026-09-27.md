# How to Tell Whether a Neutral Leans Insurer-Side or Policyholder-Side

**Prepared:** September 27, 2026 · **Case:** 27-CV-26-11606
**Not legal advice.** Method and sources verified 9/27/2026.

---

## 1. The hard signals — verifiable, and close to dispositive

### (a) MDLA membership — a signed declaration of side

The **Minnesota Defense Lawyers Association** membership application requires the applicant to certify:

> "I am an attorney **primarily involved in the defense** of civil actions in the State of Minnesota."

That is not an inference — it is a written self-declaration, renewed with each membership. **If a neutral lists MDLA anywhere in a bio, CV or LinkedIn, they have certified they were primarily a defense lawyer.** (mdla.org)

### (b) MAJ membership — the mirror image

The **Minnesota Association for Justice** describes itself as "dedicated to protecting the rights of the injured and accused," provides "plaintiff-oriented" CLE, and supports "attorneys who represent Minnesotans who have been wrongly harmed." MAJ membership means plaintiff-side. (mnaj.org)

*Neither organisation publishes a public member list, so you find this in the person's own bio — which is exactly where people list it, because both are respectable credentials.*

### (c) Who signs the paycheque now

The plainest signal of all. On the Rule 114 roster itself I found neutrals listing **@travelers.com** and **@securian.com** addresses — people currently employed by carriers. Look at the email domain and the employer line.

### (d) What the firm calls itself

Firms self-brand. Erstad & Riemer's own site title reads **"Minneapolis Insurance Defense Attorneys."** No interpretation required. Compare a firm whose site says it represents "injured people" or "policyholders."

### (e) Reported cases — free and decisive

Search the name on **Google Scholar** (scholar.google.com, Case law) or **CourtListener** (courtlistener.com). Read the captions: which side were they counsel for? Twenty minutes per candidate, and it cuts through marketing entirely.

## 2. The soft signals

| Signal | How to read it |
|---|---|
| **What they publish, and where** | Writing for insurer audiences is an insurer voice. Example from this case's research: a Robins Kaplan partner writing on *Cedar Bluff* advised that "insurers should do all they can to avoid having an appraisal panel decide such issues." That is not neutral commentary |
| **Where they speak** | MDLA panels vs. MAJ panels |
| **Self-description** | "Represented both plaintiffs and defendants" is a deliberate claim of balance — Roger Kramer says exactly this. Silence on the question, in a long bio, is itself mildly informative |
| **Who proposed them** | Opposing counsel proposing a name is a data point, not proof. Good neutrals get proposed by both sides — that is what makes them good |

## 3. Just ask — the highest-yield question in the whole process

> **"Over your career, and in the last five years, roughly what share of your work has been for policyholders versus insurers?"**

Most neutrals answer this straightforwardly; it is a fair question and they have heard it before. **A dodge is itself the answer.**

A second route: ask a policyholder-side attorney. Local reputations are well known inside that bar — **Livgard & Lloyd**, (612) 825-7777, is the call.

## 4. ⚠️ The trap — don't screen on career side alone

Ranking purely on background would be a mistake, for three reasons.

1. **Rule 114.13 subd. 1 requires impartiality**, and requires the neutral to *withdraw* if they cannot maintain it. Many excellent neutrals come from one side and are scrupulous about this.
2. **Full-time neutrals have a powerful economic reason to be fair.** Their practice depends on both sides continuing to accept them. A neutral who develops a reputation for tilting stops getting selected. That market discipline is arguably a stronger safeguard than any disclosure rule.
3. **Retired judges are the cleanest signal available** — for ten or twenty years their client was the public, not a side. That is precisely why both parties accept them, and why Bartsh, Knutson and DeCourcy rank where they do.

### The risk that actually matters for you

For a self-represented homeowner, the danger is **not** abstract pro-insurer sentiment. It is a mediator who leans on **the weaker, less-represented party** — because you are easier to move than a carrier with counsel and settlement authority. That is a question of **temperament**, not career history, and you test it differently:

- *"If one side won't move, what do you do?"*
- *"How do you handle a self-represented party?"* — a good answer raises the Rule 114.13 subd. 7 bar on giving legal advice **unprompted**.
- **Watch for anyone who evaluates your case on a first call.** A neutral offering a merits view before hearing both sides is telling you something important about how they will run the day.

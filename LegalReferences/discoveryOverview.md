# Discovery in Minnesota State District Court — An Overview

**Purpose of this document.** This is a general explainer, not a strategy memo. It describes
how discovery works in a Minnesota state civil case: what the Rule 26.06 discovery
conference is, what the discovery process itself consists of, what the deadlines are, and
where self-represented (pro se) litigants most often get into trouble. It assumes the
reader has no legal background. It deliberately avoids recommendations about what to do in
any particular case; it explains the machinery so that decisions can be made elsewhere.

**Scope.** Minnesota state district court, civil (non-family) case, governed by the
Minnesota Rules of Civil Procedure ("Minn. R. Civ. P." or "Rule ___") and the Minnesota
General Rules of Practice for the District Courts ("Gen. R. Prac."). Federal court has a
similar but *not identical* system; do not assume a federal rule number or federal
practice guide applies here. Individual judges and judicial districts also issue their own
standing orders and scheduling orders, which override the default timing in the rules.

**Last verified:** rule text checked against the Minnesota Revisor's Office as of
August 2026. Rules change. Verify anything before relying on it in a filing.

## 1. What discovery is and why it exists

Discovery is the phase of a lawsuit between the pleadings (complaint and answer) and trial
in which each side is required to hand over information to the other side. It is the
formal, court-enforceable mechanism for finding out what the other party knows, what
documents exist, and what testimony witnesses will give.

Three things about discovery surprise people who have never litigated:

**It is mostly party-driven, not court-driven.** With limited exceptions, the court does
not run discovery. The parties serve requests on each other directly, respond directly to
each other, and only involve the judge when there is a dispute. Most discovery documents
are never filed with the court at all (see §14, gotcha #1).

**It runs in both directions.** Every tool described in this document is available to the
opposing party against you. Serving interrogatories opens the door to receiving them.
Requesting the other side's internal files means producing your own emails, texts, photos,
notes, and prior claim history. A party who is not prepared to produce should not be
surprised by what discovery costs them.

**It is bounded by a clock that is easy to miss.** The court sets a "discovery cutoff"
date. Everything must be *completed* by that date, not merely started. Because a response
to a written request is not due for 30 days (plus mailing time), requests served close to
the cutoff produce nothing. Working backward from the cutoff is the single most important
scheduling habit in this phase.

The practical function of discovery is to build the evidentiary record. Claims survive or
die on summary judgment (Rule 56) based on what is in that record. A fact that is true but
was never captured in a document, an admission, or sworn testimony is, for purposes of
summary judgment, not a fact at all.

## 2. Where the rules live

Five distinct sources of rules govern discovery in a Minnesota state case. All five apply
at once, and they can conflict; when they do, the more specific and more recent control —
generally the judge's scheduling order beats the default rule.

| Source | What it governs | Where to find it |
|---|---|---|
| **Minn. R. Civ. P. 26–37, 45** | The substance of discovery: scope, disclosures, the conference, each tool, sanctions | revisor.mn.gov → Court Rules → Rules of Civil Procedure |
| **Minn. R. Civ. P. 5, 6** | Service and filing; how to count deadlines; extra days for mail | Same |
| **Gen. R. Prac. 104, 111, 115** | Civil cover sheet, scheduling orders, motion practice and its deadlines | revisor.mn.gov → Court Rules → General Rules of Practice |
| **Gen. R. Prac. 11, 14** | Restricted identifiers/redaction; eFiling and eService | Same |
| **Judicial district and judge-specific orders** | Local variations; some judges require an informal conference or letter before any discovery motion | The judicial district's website; the case's own scheduling order; the judge's chambers |

**A note on rule numbering.** Minnesota uses decimal subparts (Rule 26.06, Rule 33.01)
where the federal rules use parentheses (Fed. R. Civ. P. 26(f), 33(a)). Minnesota's Rule
26.06 conference is the analog of the federal Rule 26(f) conference. Search results,
practice guides, and AI-generated summaries frequently mix the two systems up. Always
confirm which court's rule you are reading.

## 3. The Rule 26.06 discovery conference

### 3.1 What it is

The Rule 26.06 conference — sometimes called the "meet and confer," the "discovery
planning conference," or by its federal nickname the "26(f) conference" — is a mandatory
discussion between the parties, held before formal discovery ramps up. It is **not** a
court hearing. There is no judge, no courtroom, no court reporter. It is a meeting between
the lawyers and any self-represented parties, held by phone, video, or in person.

Its purpose is to force the parties to agree in advance, if they can, on how discovery will
be conducted, and to surface disagreements early so the judge can resolve them in the
scheduling order rather than in a fight six months later.

### 3.2 When it must happen

> **Rule 26.06(a):** "Except in a proceeding exempted from initial disclosure under Rule
> 26.01(a)(2) or when the court orders otherwise, the parties must confer as soon as
> practicable — and in any event within 30 days from the initial due date for an answer."

Two features of that trigger matter:

- The clock runs from **the initial due date for an answer**, not from the date an answer
  was actually served, and not from the date of any extension. If the answer was originally
  due on a given date and the court later extended the deadline for answering, the
  extension does not move the conference deadline unless the order says so. This is a
  common trap.
- It runs from the *due date*, not from service of the summons and complaint.

### 3.3 Who is responsible for arranging it

> **Rule 26.06(b):** "The attorneys of record and all self-represented litigants that have
> appeared in the case are jointly responsible for arranging the conference, and for
> attempting in good faith to agree on the proposed discovery plan."

"Jointly responsible" means neither side can blame the other for a conference that never
happened. A party who waited for the other side to call has not complied. The practical
consequence is that the responsibility to initiate — in writing, with a paper trail — sits
on every party.

### 3.4 What is discussed

Rule 26.06(c) directs the parties to address, at minimum:

1. **Initial disclosures** — the timing and form of the Rule 26.01(a) disclosures, and
   whether any party objects to making them.
2. **Subjects of discovery** — what topics discovery will cover, whether it should be
   conducted in phases (for example, liability first, damages later), and when discovery
   should be completed.
3. **Electronically stored information (ESI)** — what electronic records exist, in what
   systems, what format they will be produced in, and who bears the cost of retrieving
   material that is hard to reach.
4. **Privilege** — how claims of privilege or work-product protection will be handled,
   including whether the parties want a "claw-back" agreement (a mechanism for retrieving
   privileged material that is produced by accident) entered as a court order.
5. **Changes to the default limits** — whether the presumptive limits on discovery should
   be increased or decreased (for example, more than 50 interrogatories, or a cap on
   depositions).
6. **Any other orders** the court should enter — protective orders, confidentiality
   designations, scheduling requests.

In practice, an experienced opposing lawyer will arrive with a draft plan. A party who
arrives without one will typically end up negotiating against the other side's document.

### 3.5 What the conference is not

- It is **not** the meet-and-confer required before filing a discovery motion. That is a
  separate, later obligation under Rule 37.01 and Gen. R. Prac. 115.10, and it must be
  satisfied again each time a motion is contemplated.
- It is **not** a settlement conference, although settlement and alternative dispute
  resolution (ADR) are often discussed because the scheduling order must address ADR.
- It is **not** optional, and it is not satisfied by a single unanswered email — though a
  documented series of unanswered attempts to schedule it is exactly the record needed if
  the failure has to be explained to the court later.

### 3.6 If the other side will not participate

The rule imposes a joint duty, so a party facing a non-responsive opponent generally
documents the attempts (dated letters and emails, with copies kept), then reports the
impasse to the court — typically by filing a unilateral discovery plan that states what was
proposed, when, and what response was received, or by moving for a scheduling conference.
The record of attempts is what distinguishes a party who complied from one who did not.

## 4. The written discovery plan

### 4.1 The deadline

> **Rule 26.06(b):** the parties must submit to the court a written report outlining the
> plan **"within 14 days after the conference or at the time the action is filed, whichever
> is later."**

So: hold the conference, then file the report within 14 days.

### 4.2 What it looks like

The report is a short document — typically two to five pages — filed with the court. It
recites that the conference occurred, on what date, and who participated, and then walks
through the Rule 26.06(c) topics, stating for each either the parties' agreement or their
competing positions.

A *joint* plan is the norm and is what courts prefer. Where the parties disagree, the plan
sets out both positions side by side so the judge can pick. Where one party will not
participate, the other files a unilateral plan explaining why.

### 4.3 Its relationship to the civil cover sheet and the scheduling order

Under Gen. R. Prac. 111.02, parties may also submit scheduling input to the court as part
of the **civil cover sheet** required by Gen. R. Prac. 104. Some judicial districts and
individual judges additionally request an "informational statement" or a completed
scheduling worksheet. These are separate documents from the Rule 26.06 report, and a
district's practice may require one, both, or a combined filing. The case's own scheduling
notices and the district's website are the authority on which format is expected.

Whatever form it takes, the plan is an input. The output is the scheduling order (§6),
which is what actually binds the parties.

## 5. Initial disclosures (Rule 26.01(a))

Minnesota, like federal court, requires each party to hand over certain core information
**without being asked**. This is a change from older practice, and parties who learned
litigation before the rule was adopted sometimes miss it entirely.

### 5.1 What must be disclosed

| Category | What it means in practice |
|---|---|
| **Witnesses** | The name, and if known the address and telephone number, of each individual likely to have discoverable information the disclosing party may use to support its claims or defenses. Note: individuals *you* may use — not every person who knows something. |
| **Documents and ESI** | A copy of, or a description by category and location of, all documents, electronically stored information, and tangible things the disclosing party has in its possession, custody, or control and may use to support its claims or defenses. |
| **Damages computation** | A computation of each category of damages claimed, plus the documents on which the computation is based, made available for inspection. A number without a supporting calculation is an incomplete disclosure. |
| **Insurance** | Any insurance agreement under which an insurer may be liable to satisfy all or part of a judgment. |

### 5.2 Timing

> **Rule 26.01(a):** "A party must make the initial disclosures at or within 60 days after
> the original due date when an answer is required," unless modified by stipulation, court
> order, or an objection stated in the discovery plan.

Again the trigger is the **original** answer due date. A party joined later in the case
discloses within 30 days of being served.

### 5.3 The "no excuses" clause

> "A party is not excused from making its disclosures because it has not fully investigated
> the case or because it challenges the sufficiency of another party's disclosures."

You cannot withhold your disclosures because the other side withheld theirs, and you cannot
delay because your own investigation is incomplete. You disclose what you have and
supplement later (§9.4).

### 5.4 Exemptions

Rule 26.01(a)(2) exempts certain categories of proceedings from initial disclosure —
administrative review, forfeitures, habeas, family court matters, conciliation court
appeals, and others. An ordinary civil contract or tort case in district court is **not**
exempt.

## 6. The scheduling order (Gen. R. Prac. 111.03)

### 6.1 What it is

The scheduling order is the judge's order that fixes the case's calendar. Once it issues,
it is the controlling deadline document for the rest of the case — more important, in
day-to-day terms, than the rules themselves, because it overrides the rules' default dates.

> **Gen. R. Prac. 111.03:** "No sooner than the due date of the last civil cover sheet
> under Rule 104, and no longer than 90 days after an action has been filed, the court
> shall enter its scheduling order."

### 6.2 What it typically contains

- Deadline to join additional parties or amend the pleadings
- **Discovery cutoff** — the date by which all discovery must be *completed*
- Expert disclosure and expert deposition deadlines (often earlier than the Rule 26.01(b)
  default)
- Deadline to file non-dispositive motions (discovery motions live here)
- Deadline to file dispositive motions (summary judgment)
- Alternative dispute resolution requirement — which ADR process, and by when
- Trial-ready date or trial date, expected trial length, jury or court trial
- Whether the case is on a "standard" or "complex" track

### 6.3 Changing it

> **Gen. R. Prac. 111.04:** "A scheduling order pursuant to this rule may be amended at a
> pretrial conference or upon motion for good cause shown."

"Good cause" is a real standard, not a formality. Courts routinely grant a first,
stipulated, timely-requested extension and are markedly less receptive to a request made
after the deadline has passed, or one caused by a party's own delay. The practical rule is
that scheduling relief is asked for *before* the deadline, not after.

## 7. The scope of discovery — what is discoverable

### 7.1 The basic test

> **Rule 26.02(b):** parties may obtain discovery of "any nonprivileged matter that is
> relevant to any party's claim or defense and proportional to the needs of the case."

Two independent requirements: **relevant** and **proportional**. Both must be satisfied.

Relevance in discovery is broader than relevance at trial. Information does not have to be
admissible to be discoverable; it is enough that it is relevant to a claim or defense. But
the older formulation — "reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible
evidence" — is no longer the standard, and citing it signals unfamiliarity with the current
rule.

### 7.2 Proportionality factors

Rule 26.02(b) directs consideration of:

- the importance of the issues at stake in the action
- the amount in controversy
- the parties' relative access to relevant information
- the parties' resources
- the importance of the discovery in resolving the issues
- whether the burden or expense of the proposed discovery outweighs its likely benefit

Note the third factor. **"The parties' relative access to relevant information"** is the
factor that addresses information asymmetry — the situation where one side holds nearly all
the records. It cuts in favor of the party that does not have the documents.

Note also the fourth factor. "The parties' resources" is not a one-way ratchet in favor of
the smaller party; it is a factor a well-resourced opponent will also invoke, arguing that
a request is disproportionate to what the case is worth.

### 7.3 Electronically stored information

> **Rule 26.02:** information "not reasonably accessible because of undue burden or cost"
> need not be produced unless the requesting party shows good cause and proportionality.

A responding party can decline to produce hard-to-reach ESI — backup tapes, deleted data
requiring forensic recovery, legacy systems — by identifying it as not reasonably
accessible. The requesting party can then move to compel and must justify the burden.

> **Rule 34:** "If a request does not specify the form for producing electronically stored
> information, a responding party must produce the information in a form or forms in which
> it is ordinarily maintained or in a reasonably usable form."

This is why requests routinely specify format. Without a specification, a producing party
may satisfy the rule with flat PDFs or paper printouts that strip out metadata (dates,
authors, revision history, embedded fields) — often the most probative part of an
electronic record.

### 7.4 Work product and privilege

- **Attorney-client privilege** protects confidential communications between a client and
  a lawyer for the purpose of legal advice. It is not lost merely because a document is
  useful to the other side, and it is lost if the communication is shared with outsiders.
- **Work product** (Rule 26.02(e)) protects materials prepared in anticipation of
  litigation. It is a qualified protection: an opponent can overcome it by showing
  "substantial need" and an inability to obtain the equivalent by other means. But the
  court "must protect against disclosure of the mental impressions, conclusions, opinions,
  or legal theories" of a party's representative — that core is close to absolute.
- A self-represented party has no attorney-client privilege in the usual sense (there is no
  attorney), but does have work product protection for materials prepared for the
  litigation, and other privileges (spousal, medical, etc.) apply normally.

### 7.5 Sequence

> **Rule 26.04:** discovery methods may be used "in any sequence" unless the court orders
> otherwise, and Rule 34 document requests may be served before the mandatory initial
> conference.

There is no rule requiring one side to go first, and no rule that a party may delay its
own responses because it served its requests first.

## 8. The discovery tools

Six main instruments. Each has its own rule, its own limits, and its own deadline.

### 8.1 Interrogatories (Rule 33)

**What they are.** Written questions served on another *party*, answered in writing and
under oath. They cannot be served on non-parties.

| Feature | Rule |
|---|---|
| Numerical limit | "No party may serve more than a total of 50 interrogatories upon any other party unless permitted to do so by the court upon motion." |
| Subparts | "In computing the total number of interrogatories each subdivision of separate questions shall be counted as an interrogatory." |
| When they may be served | "without leave of court, be served upon any party after service of the summons and complaint" |
| Deadline to respond | 30 days after service — **except** a defendant may respond within 45 days after service of the summons and complaint, whichever is later |
| Form of objection | "Objections shall state with particularity the grounds for the objection" |
| Verification | Answers are made under oath; a signed verification page is part of a complete response |

**What they are good for.** Identifying people (who inspected, who decided, who signed
off), identifying documents and systems, pinning down the factual basis for a pleaded
defense, and getting a party's position on the record in a form it cannot later disavow
without explanation.

**What they are bad for.** Anything requiring nuance or follow-up. Interrogatory answers are
drafted by opposing counsel, not by the witness, and come back narrow, qualified, and
carefully worded. They do not substitute for a deposition.

**The 50-count trap.** Because every discrete subpart counts, a question phrased as
"Identify each inspection, including the date, the inspector, the scope, and the findings"
may be counted by the opposing party as four interrogatories, not one. Parties routinely
disagree about the count, and the disagreement itself becomes a discovery dispute. Numbering
questions conservatively and tracking a running total is the standard defense.

### 8.2 Requests for production of documents and things (Rule 34)

**What they are.** Written requests that another party produce documents, ESI, or tangible
things for inspection and copying, or permit entry onto land or property for inspection,
measuring, photographing, or testing.

| Feature | Rule |
|---|---|
| Numerical limit | None in the rules |
| When they may be served | "with or after service of the summons and complaint" |
| Deadline to respond | 30 days after service (defendant: not before 45 days after service of the summons and complaint) |
| Required content of response | For each item or category, state either that inspection will be permitted as requested, or "state with specificity the grounds for objecting to the request, including the reasons" |
| Withholding | "An objection must state whether any responsive materials are being withheld on the basis of that objection." |

That last requirement is significant and frequently ignored. A responding party may not
object, produce a partial set, and leave the requesting party guessing whether anything was
held back. The objection must say so.

**Practical mechanics.** Requests should define terms, specify the relevant time period,
and specify production format for ESI. Responses are typically served as a written document
with a separate production of files, Bates-numbered (a sequential label stamped on each page
so documents can be cited unambiguously later).

**Property inspection.** Rule 34 also covers entry onto property. This is the mechanism for
a party's expert to examine physical evidence at a site.

### 8.3 Requests for admission (Rule 36)

**What they are.** Written statements the other party must admit or deny. They cover the
truth of facts, the application of law to fact, opinions of fact, and — importantly — the
genuineness of documents.

| Feature | Rule |
|---|---|
| Numerical limit | None in the rules |
| Deadline to respond | 30 days after service (defendant: not required to respond before 45 days after service of the summons and complaint) |
| **Failure to respond** | "The matter is admitted unless within 30 days after service of the request … the party to whom the request is directed serves upon the party requesting the admission a written answer or objection." |
| Form of denial | Must "specifically deny the matter or set forth in detail the reasons why the answering party cannot truthfully admit or deny," and must "fairly meet the substance of the requested admission" |
| Lack of knowledge | A party asserting it cannot admit or deny must state "that a reasonable inquiry has been made" |
| Effect | Admitted matters are "conclusively established unless the court on motion permits withdrawal or amendment" (Rule 36.02) |
| Scope of the admission | Applies to "the pending action only" — not usable in any other proceeding |

**The automatic-admission rule is the sharpest edge in all of discovery.** No motion is
required. No court order is required. A party that misses the 30-day window has admitted
every request served on it, and getting out requires a motion under Rule 36.02 that the
court is not obligated to grant. This cuts in both directions and is worth calendaring the
moment any request for admission arrives.

**Authenticating documents.** The cheapest way to make an exhibit usable at summary judgment
or trial is a request for admission that the document is genuine. Without authentication, a
document is just paper; an opponent can object to it and force a foundation witness.

### 8.4 Depositions (Rule 30)

**What they are.** Live, sworn, oral questioning of a witness — a party or a non-party —
recorded by a court reporter, with a transcript produced afterward. The most expensive and
most powerful tool.

| Feature | Rule |
|---|---|
| Leave of court | Required only "if the plaintiff seeks to take a deposition prior to the expiration of 30 days after service of the summons and complaint upon any defendant" — and not required if a defendant has already initiated discovery |
| Numerical limit | **Minnesota's rules do not set a numeric cap** (unlike federal court's 10-deposition presumptive limit). The scheduling order or a stipulated plan may impose one. |
| Length | "Unless otherwise authorized by the court or stipulated by the parties, a deposition is limited to one day of seven hours" (Rule 30.04(b)). Courts must allow more time where needed for a fair examination. |
| Notice | Must state the name and place for the deposition, the name and address of each person to be examined, and "whether the deposition shall be conducted in person or by remote technology" |
| Objections | "Any objection to evidence during a deposition shall be stated concisely and in a non-argumentative and non-suggestive manner" (Rule 30.04(a)) |
| Sanctions | Available for conduct that frustrates a fair examination (Rule 30.04(c)) |

**Organizational depositions — Rule 30.02(f).** A party may notice the deposition of an
entity rather than a named individual, and "must describe with reasonable particularity the
matters for examination." The organization "must designate one or more officers, directors,
or managing agents" to testify on those topics. This is the mechanism for getting binding
testimony from a corporation about its own policies, systems, and decisions without knowing
in advance which employee has the answer. The designated witness's testimony binds the
entity as to the noticed topics, which is why the topic list is drafted carefully and
opposing counsel usually negotiates over it.

**Cost.** A deposition requires a court reporter (hourly plus transcript fees, commonly
several hundred to a few thousand dollars per deposition), a location, and often a
videographer. The taking party generally bears these costs. This is why depositions are
usually taken after written discovery has narrowed what needs to be asked.

**Non-party depositions** require a subpoena (§8.5).

### 8.5 Subpoenas to non-parties (Rule 45)

**What they are.** The mechanism for obtaining documents or testimony from someone who is
not a party to the lawsuit — an independent contractor, a prior vendor, a records
custodian, a treating professional, a former employee.

| Feature | Rule |
|---|---|
| Who issues | "The court administrator shall issue a subpoena, signed but otherwise in blank, to a party requesting it"; also "[a]n attorney as officer of the court may also issue and sign a subpoena on behalf of the court" (Rule 45.01(c)). The advisory committee comment states that the attorney-issuance authority "is not extended to self-represented litigants" — so a pro se party obtains subpoenas from the court administrator. |
| **Notice to other parties** | "Any use of a subpoena, other than to compel attendance at a trial, without prior notice to all parties to the action, is improper" (Rule 45.01(e)). Notice must be served under Rule 5.02 on each party "at least seven days before the required production" (Rule 45.02(a)). |
| Geographic reach | "A subpoena may be served at any place within the state of Minnesota." Non-parties generally cannot be made to travel outside their county except to attend trial. |
| Fees | The serving party must tender "the fees for one day's attendance and the mileage allowed by law" |
| Objection window | The recipient may object "[w]ithin 14 days after service of the subpoena or before the time specified for compliance if such time is less than 14 days" (Rule 45.03(b)(2)) |
| Duty to limit burden | The issuing party "shall take reasonable steps to avoid imposing undue burden or expense"; sanctions available for breach (Rule 45.03(a)) |

The seven-day advance-notice requirement is the most-missed part of Rule 45. Serving a
document subpoena on a third party without first notifying every other party is improper on
the face of the rule, and it hands the opposing party a clean procedural objection
regardless of the merits of the request.

### 8.6 Physical and mental examinations, and other tools

Rule 35 permits court-ordered physical or mental examinations where a party's condition is
in controversy. Rule 31 permits depositions on written questions (rare). Rule 34 property
inspection is described above.

## 9. Responding to discovery served on you

### 9.1 The clock

Written discovery responses are due **30 days after service** (Rules 33.01, 34.02, 36.01),
with the defendant's floor of 45 days after service of the summons and complaint for
requests served very early.

**Deadlines run from service, not from receipt.** Under Rule 5.02, "[s]ervice by mail is
complete upon mailing." A packet mailed on the 1st and received on the 5th started running
on the 1st.

**Rule 6.01(e) adds three days for mail.** "Whenever a party has the right or is required to
do some act … within a prescribed period after the service of a notice or other document
upon the party, and the notice or document is served upon the party by United States Mail,
3 days shall be added to the prescribed period." One additional day is added if service is
made after 5:00 p.m. Minnesota time by means other than U.S. Mail.

**Rule 6.01 counting.** Exclude the day of the triggering event; count every day including
weekends and holidays; if the last day is a weekend or legal holiday, the period runs to the
next business day. For periods shorter than seven days, weekends and holidays are excluded
only if another rule says so.

### 9.2 What a complete response looks like

- **Interrogatories:** separate written answers to each numbered interrogatory, or a
  particularized objection, signed and verified under oath.
- **Document requests:** a written response addressing each request individually, stating
  either that production will be made or the specific grounds for objection — and stating
  whether anything is being withheld under that objection — plus the actual production.
- **Requests for admission:** an admission, a specific denial, an explanation of why the
  party cannot truthfully admit or deny (with a statement that reasonable inquiry was made),
  or an objection.

### 9.3 Answering *and* objecting

Objecting to part of a request does not excuse answering the rest. The standard structure
is to state the objection, then answer "subject to and without waiving" it, so that the
objection is preserved but the responding party is not accused of stonewalling. A response
that consists only of objections invites a motion to compel and the associated fee-shifting.

### 9.4 The continuing duty to supplement (Rule 26.05)

> Parties must supplement disclosures and responses "in a timely manner if the party learns
> that in some material respect the disclosure or response is incomplete or incorrect."

This is a **continuing** obligation. A response that was accurate in October and inaccurate
in February must be corrected. For experts, the duty extends to information in the report
and the expert's deposition, and supplementation is due by the time pretrial disclosures are
required.

The practical significance is at trial: a witness or exhibit that was never disclosed, or a
damages number that changed without supplementation, is exposed to a motion to exclude.

### 9.5 Pretrial disclosures (Rule 26.01(c))

Separate from initial disclosures and expert disclosures, each party must, **at least 30
days before trial**, identify the witnesses it expects to call (distinguishing those it will
call from those it may call if needed), designate any deposition testimony to be used, and
identify each exhibit. Objections to admissibility are due "within 14 days after they are
made," and objections not made are generally waived except under the evidence rules.

---

## 10. Objections, privilege, and protective orders

### 10.1 Legitimate objections

- **Relevance** — the request does not bear on any claim or defense.
- **Proportionality / burden** — the effort or expense outweighs the likely benefit, judged
  by the Rule 26.02(b) factors.
- **Privilege / work product** — see §7.4.
- **Vagueness or ambiguity** — the request cannot be understood well enough to answer;
  usually paired with an answer based on a stated interpretation.
- **Overbreadth as to time or subject** — usually paired with an offer to produce within a
  narrower defined scope.
- **Already produced / equally available** — including the Rule 33.03 option to produce
  business records instead of answering an interrogatory, available where "the burden of
  deriving or ascertaining the answer is substantially the same" for both parties.

### 10.2 Boilerplate objections

Reflexive strings of general objections ("vague, ambiguous, overbroad, unduly burdensome,
not reasonably calculated…") appended to every request are disfavored. Rule 33.01 requires
objections to "state with particularity the grounds," and Rule 34.02 requires specificity
plus a statement of whether material is withheld. Boilerplate is both a losing position when
asserted and, when received, the basis for a motion to compel.

### 10.3 Privilege logs

> **Rule 26.02(f):** a party asserting privilege must "describe the nature of the documents,
> communications, or things not produced" in a way that enables other parties to assess the
> claim, without revealing the privileged content itself.

In practice this is a **privilege log** — a table listing each withheld document with its
date, author, recipients, general subject, and the privilege asserted. Withholding documents
without logging them risks a finding that the privilege was waived.

### 10.4 Inadvertent production and claw-back

Rule 26.02(f) also provides that if privileged information is produced by accident, the
receiving party must promptly return, sequester, or destroy it on notice. Parties often
formalize this in a claw-back agreement entered as a court order during the Rule 26.06
conference, precisely so that an accidental production does not become a waiver fight.

### 10.5 Protective orders (Rule 26.03)

A party from whom discovery is sought may move for a protective order "for good cause
shown." Relief can include forbidding the discovery, limiting its scope, specifying terms,
designating material confidential, or sealing. A motion for a protective order carries the
same good-faith-conference requirement as a motion to compel.

Protective orders are also the standard vehicle for producing genuinely sensitive material —
trade secrets, personnel files, financial records — under a confidentiality designation
rather than fighting about whether to produce it at all.

## 11. Expert discovery

Expert testimony is discovered on its own track, and its deadlines are usually the tightest
in the case.

### 11.1 What must be disclosed (Rule 26.01(b))

Every testifying expert must be identified. For an expert retained specifically to provide
testimony, or whose regular duties involve giving expert testimony, a written report is
required containing:

- "a complete statement of all opinions the witness will express and the basis and reasons"
- the facts or data considered
- the expert's qualifications
- publications authored in the previous 10 years
- a list of other cases in which the expert testified in the previous 4 years
- the compensation to be paid

### 11.2 Timing

> **Rule 26.01(b):** disclosures must be made "at least 90 days before the date set for
> trial," or within 30 days after another party's disclosure if the evidence is intended
> solely to rebut that party's expert.

**The scheduling order almost always overrides this**, and almost always sets earlier dates —
often staggered, with the party bearing the burden of proof disclosing first, the responding
party disclosing some weeks later, and expert depositions after that. The 90-day rule is the
floor when the scheduling order is silent, not the operative date in most cases.

### 11.3 Why the timing matters more than it looks

An expert disclosure deadline set months before the discovery cutoff means the expert must
have the underlying records *before* that deadline. If the records are coming from the
opposing party through document requests, the sequence is: serve requests → 30 days plus
mailing to respond → resolve any deficiencies → produce records to the expert → expert
prepares report → disclose. That chain typically needs three to four months of runway. A
party that serves its document requests late may find its expert deadline arrives before its
evidence does.

### 11.4 Consulting versus testifying experts

An expert retained purely to advise, who will not testify, is generally shielded from
discovery. An expert who is disclosed as a testifying witness is not: the report, the facts
considered, and the expert's file typically become discoverable, and the expert is subject
to deposition. The decision to convert a consultant into a testifying witness is therefore a
decision to open that person's work to the other side.

## 12. Enforcement: meet and confer, motions to compel, sanctions

### 12.1 The mandatory conference before any motion

Two separate rules require it:

> **Rule 37.01:** a motion to compel must include "a certification that the movant has in
> good faith conferred or attempted to confer with the person or party failing to make the
> discovery."

> **Gen. R. Prac. 115.10:** "No motion will be heard unless the parties have conferred in an
> attempt to resolve their differences prior to the hearing. The moving party shall initiate
> the conference." The moving party must certify compliance, or explain why it was not
> possible, before the hearing date.

A motion filed without a real conference is subject to denial on that ground alone,
regardless of merit. "Conferred" means an actual attempt to resolve the dispute — typically a
detailed letter identifying each deficient response and the reason, followed by a call or
meeting. A single email saying "your responses are inadequate" is thin.

### 12.2 Motion practice deadlines (Gen. R. Prac. 115)

Discovery motions are **non-dispositive** motions:

| Filing | Deadline before the hearing |
|---|---|
| Moving party's motion, proposed order, affidavits/exhibits, memorandum | at least **21 days** before the hearing (non-dispositive) |
| Responding party's documents | at least **14 days** before the hearing |
| Reply memorandum (limited to new matters raised in the response) | at least **7 days** before the hearing |

For **dispositive** motions (summary judgment, motions to dismiss) the moving party's
deadline is **28 days** before the hearing; response 14 days; reply 7 days.

Memoranda of law are capped at 35 pages absent permission (Gen. R. Prac. 115.05).

> **Gen. R. Prac. 115.06:** "If the moving documents are not properly served and filed, the
> hearing may be canceled by the court. If responsive documents are not properly served and
> filed in a nondispositive motion, the court may deem the motion unopposed and may grant
> the relief requested without a hearing."

That last sentence is worth reading twice. Failing to respond to the other side's discovery
motion can result in the relief being granted without argument.

Many judges and districts require a party to request an informal telephone conference, or
submit a short letter, before filing a discovery motion at all. Check the judge's practices
before drafting a motion.

### 12.3 What a motion to compel can obtain

An order compelling answers, compelling production, overruling objections, setting a
deadline for compliance, and awarding expenses.

> **Rule 37.01:** "an evasive or incomplete disclosure, answer, or response is to be treated
> as a failure to disclose, answer, or respond."

### 12.4 Fee-shifting

> When a motion to compel is granted, "the court **shall** … require the party or deponent
> whose conduct necessitated the motion or the party or attorney advising such conduct or
> both of them to pay to the moving party the reasonable expenses incurred in making the
> motion, including attorney fees."

Note "shall," subject to the rule's exceptions (the opposition was substantially justified,
or the movant filed without first conferring in good faith, or other circumstances make an
award unjust). Fee-shifting runs both directions: a party that files an unjustified motion
can be ordered to pay the responding party's expenses. A self-represented party who wins a
motion to compel generally recovers out-of-pocket costs but typically not "attorney fees,"
since none were incurred.

### 12.5 Escalating sanctions (Rule 37.02)

For violating a discovery order, the court may, among other things:

- deem designated facts established
- prohibit the disobedient party from supporting or opposing designated claims or defenses
- prohibit introduction of designated matters into evidence
- strike pleadings in whole or in part
- stay proceedings until the order is obeyed
- dismiss the action, or enter judgment by default against the disobedient party
- treat the failure as contempt

These are ordered against parties, including self-represented ones. Dismissal for discovery
misconduct is real.

### 12.6 Failure to attend or respond at all (Rule 37.04)

If a party fails to appear for its own deposition or serve answers to interrogatories after
proper service, the court "on motion may make such orders in regard to the failure as are
just" and "shall require the party failing to act … to pay the reasonable expenses,
including attorney fees, caused by the failure." Note that failing to respond at all is
treated more harshly than responding with objections, because the rule does not require the
non-responding party to have first been ordered to comply.

### 12.7 Lost electronic evidence (Rule 37.05)

If ESI that should have been preserved is lost because a party failed to take reasonable
steps, the court may order measures to cure the prejudice. But the severe sanctions — an
adverse-inference instruction, dismissal, or default — require a finding "that the party
acted with the intent to deprive another party of the information's use in the litigation."

The corollary is the **litigation hold**: once litigation is reasonably anticipated, a party
must stop routine deletion of relevant material. This obligation applies to individual
plaintiffs as well as institutions — text messages, emails, photos on a phone, notes,
voicemails, and social media posts are all potentially subject to it.

## 13. The discovery timeline as a whole

### 13.1 The default sequence

| Stage | Default timing |
|---|---|
| Answer served | 21 days after service of summons and complaint (Rule 12.01), subject to extensions |
| **Rule 26.06 discovery conference** | within **30 days from the initial answer due date** |
| **Written discovery plan filed** | within **14 days after the conference**, or at filing of the action, whichever is later |
| **Rule 26.01(a) initial disclosures** | at or within **60 days after the original answer due date** |
| **Scheduling order issues** | no later than **90 days after the action was filed** |
| Written discovery served / responded | 30 days per round (+3 for mail), any time before the cutoff allows |
| Fact depositions | typically after written discovery; before the cutoff |
| Expert disclosures | per the scheduling order; default floor is 90 days before trial |
| **Discovery cutoff** | set by the scheduling order |
| Dispositive motions | per the scheduling order; not more than 30 days after the close of all discovery (Rule 56.02); served at least 28 days before the hearing under Gen. R. Prac. 115.03 |
| Pretrial disclosures | at least 30 days before trial (Rule 26.01(c)) |

### 13.2 Planning backward from the cutoff

Because responses take 30 days plus mailing, and because disputes take longer still, the
last useful dates for each activity are considerably earlier than the cutoff:

- **Last day to serve written discovery** and still get a response before the cutoff:
  roughly **35–40 days** before the cutoff (30-day response + 3 days for mail + margin).
- **Last day to serve written discovery** and still leave room to meet and confer *and*
  bring a motion to compel: add the conference period plus the 21-day motion notice plus
  hearing scheduling — realistically **90–120 days** before the cutoff.
- **Last day to notice a deposition** — the deposition itself must occur before the cutoff,
  and calendars for witnesses, counsel, and court reporters are usually booked weeks out.
- **Documents needed by an expert** must be in hand well before the expert disclosure
  deadline, which is itself usually before the discovery cutoff.

### 13.3 Extensions

Extensions of discovery deadlines require either a stipulation approved by the court or a
motion under Gen. R. Prac. 111.04 for good cause. Rule 6.02 allows the court to extend
deadlines generally — in its discretion if the request comes before expiration, but only on
a showing of **excusable neglect** if the request comes after expiration.

### 13.4 The one-year filing rule (Rule 5.04)

> "Any action that is not filed with the court within one year of commencement against any
> party is deemed dismissed with prejudice against all parties unless the parties within
> that year sign a stipulation to extend the filing period."

In Minnesota an action is *commenced* by service, and *filed* separately. This rule is about
the gap between the two and is unrelated to discovery deadlines, but it appears here because
it is the single most severe automatic deadline in Minnesota civil practice and is
occasionally confused with discovery timing.

## 14. Common gotchas for pro se litigants

Ordered roughly by how often they cause real damage.

**1. Filing discovery with the court.** Rule 5.04 provides that "disclosures under Rule 26,
expert disclosures and reports, depositions upon oral examination and interrogatories,
requests for documents, requests for admission, and answers and responses thereto shall not
be filed unless authorized by court order or rule." Discovery is *served* on the other party,
not filed. Court administrators may reject a filing that contains unauthorized discovery
requests. (Exception: discovery documents attached as exhibits to a motion are properly
filed as part of that motion.)

**2. Missing a request-for-admission deadline.** Automatic, self-executing, no motion
required, and potentially case-ending. Calendar it the day the requests arrive.

**3. Confusing the initial answer due date with the extended one.** The Rule 26.06 conference
deadline and the Rule 26.01(a) disclosure deadline both run from the *original* answer due
date. An extension of time to answer does not, by itself, move either one.

**4. Counting deadlines from receipt instead of service.** Service by mail is complete upon
mailing (Rule 5.02). The extra three days under Rule 6.01(e) are added to the *period*, not
substituted for the days already lost in transit.

**5. Treating the conference as optional or as the other side's job.** Rule 26.06(b) makes it
a joint responsibility of "all self-represented litigants that have appeared." Failure to
initiate is a failure to comply.

**6. Not preserving your own material.** The litigation hold applies to plaintiffs. Deleting
text messages, letting a phone auto-purge photos, discarding a damaged item, throwing away
notes, or continuing to overwrite files after litigation is anticipated all create exposure
under Rule 37.05 — and hand the other side an argument about what was destroyed.

**7. Underestimating what discovery costs you, not just them.** Serving broad requests
invites broad requests in return. Every category requested is a category that will be
requested back. Expect to produce your own emails, financial records, prior claims history,
communications with anyone about the dispute, and any social media touching on it.

**8. Boilerplate objections, or objections without answers.** Both rules require
particularity, and Rule 34.02 requires stating whether material is withheld. A response that
is all objection and no substance is the easiest motion to compel an opponent will ever
file, and fee-shifting under Rule 37.01 is mandatory when such a motion is granted absent
substantial justification.

**9. Withholding documents without a privilege log.** Rule 26.02(f) requires describing what
is withheld. Silent withholding risks waiver.

**10. Serving a non-party subpoena without notifying the other parties.** Rule 45.01(e)
declares it improper on its face, and Rule 45.02(a) requires notice at least seven days
before production is due.

**11. Skipping the meet-and-confer before a motion.** Required twice over by Rule 37.01 and
Gen. R. Prac. 115.10. A motion filed without it can be denied without reaching the merits,
and the movant can be ordered to pay the other side's expenses.

**12. Missing the 21-day non-dispositive motion deadline.** Discovery motions must be served
and filed at least 21 days before the hearing (Gen. R. Prac. 115.04). Filing later means the
hearing can be canceled (115.06).

**13. Not responding to the other side's non-dispositive motion.** Gen. R. Prac. 115.06 lets
the court "deem the motion unopposed and … grant the relief requested without a hearing."

**14. Waiting too long to move to compel.** There is no automatic extension of the discovery
cutoff while a dispute is pending. A motion brought a month before the cutoff may be heard
after it, by which point the requested discovery is out of time.

**15. Assuming a dispositive motion pauses discovery.** There is no automatic stay in
Minnesota. A party facing a summary judgment motion filed before discovery is complete must
affirmatively invoke **Rule 56.04**, which permits a nonmovant to show "by affidavit that,
for specified reasons, it cannot present facts essential to justify its opposition," after
which the court may defer or deny the motion, allow time for discovery, or issue any other
appropriate order. The affidavit must identify the specific facts sought and why they are
essential — a general complaint that discovery is unfinished is not enough.

**16. Interrogatory counting.** 50 total, with every discrete subpart counted separately
(Rule 33.01). A compound question can consume five of the 50. Track the running count.

**17. Forgetting the verification page.** Interrogatory answers are made under oath.
Unsigned or unverified answers are incomplete responses.

**18. Not asking for ESI in a usable format.** Absent a specified format, a producing party
may produce "in a form or forms in which it is ordinarily maintained or in a reasonably
usable form" (Rule 34) — which can mean flat PDFs stripped of metadata. Format is specified
in the request, or negotiated at the Rule 26.06 conference, or not at all.

**19. Assuming discovery responses are evidence.** They are not, automatically. Winning a
summary judgment motion requires admissible material: affidavits or declarations, deposition
transcript excerpts, authenticated exhibits, and admissions under Rule 36. A document
produced in discovery still needs authentication — which is exactly what a request for
admission of genuineness provides cheaply.

**20. Restricted identifiers.** Gen. R. Prac. 11 restricts filing documents containing
Social Security numbers, financial account numbers, and similar identifiers without proper
redaction and a cover form. This applies to discovery material attached as an exhibit to a
motion. A noncompliant filing can be stricken, and a document refiled later is generally
treated as filed on the refiling date unless the original date is restored by motion.

**21. Communicating with a represented opposing party.** Once counsel appears, communication
about the case goes to counsel, not to the party, its employees, or its adjusters. This
applies to self-represented litigants as a matter of professional and practical norms and,
separately, because informal contact produces no usable record.

**22. Serving the wrong entity.** Corporate families often have multiple named entities.
Discovery served on an entity that is not a party is a nullity; information held by a
non-party affiliate is obtained by subpoena under Rule 45, not by Rule 33 or 34 requests.

**23. No proof of service.** Every discovery document served needs a certificate or affidavit
of service, retained in your own file. When a dispute arises about whether something was
served and when, the certificate is the answer.

**24. Expecting procedural leniency.** Minnesota courts extend some practical accommodation
to self-represented parties, but the settled rule is that they are held to substantially the
same standards as attorneys. Missing a deadline because the rule was unfamiliar is not
generally excusable neglect.

**25. Relying on federal rules, out-of-state guides, or unverified AI output.** Federal Rule
26(f) and Minnesota Rule 26.06 are similar but differ in detail; the federal 10-deposition
cap does not exist in Minnesota's rules; the Minnesota 50-interrogatory limit differs from
the federal 25. Rule text should be read at the source (revisor.mn.gov) before it is relied
on in a filing.

**26. Not reading the scheduling order after it issues.** It supersedes the defaults. Every
date in §13.1 is provisional until the scheduling order fixes the real one.

## 15. Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Bates numbering** | Sequential labels stamped on produced pages so any document can be cited unambiguously |
| **Claw-back** | An agreement or order allowing return of privileged material produced by accident, without waiver |
| **Deemed admitted** | The automatic effect under Rule 36.01 when a request for admission is not answered in time |
| **Discovery cutoff** | The scheduling-order date by which all discovery must be completed |
| **Dispositive motion** | A motion that could end all or part of the case (summary judgment, dismissal) |
| **ESI** | Electronically stored information — emails, files, database records, texts, metadata |
| **Fee-shifting** | An order requiring the losing party on a motion to pay the winner's expenses |
| **Litigation hold** | The duty to stop routine deletion of potentially relevant material once litigation is anticipated |
| **Meet and confer** | The required good-faith attempt to resolve a dispute before bringing it to the judge |
| **Non-dispositive motion** | A procedural motion that does not end the case (most discovery motions) |
| **Privilege log** | The itemized description of documents withheld on privilege grounds |
| **Pro se / self-represented** | A party appearing without a lawyer |
| **Proportionality** | The Rule 26.02(b) limit weighing the burden of discovery against its likely benefit |
| **Protective order** | A court order limiting or conditioning discovery, often imposing confidentiality |
| **Rule 30.02(f) deposition** | Deposition of an organization on noticed topics, through a designated witness |
| **Service** | Formal delivery of a document to another party; complete upon mailing under Rule 5.02 |
| **Supplementation** | The continuing duty under Rule 26.05 to correct incomplete or inaccurate responses |
| **Verification** | The sworn signature attesting that interrogatory answers are true |
| **Work product** | Qualified protection for material prepared in anticipation of litigation |

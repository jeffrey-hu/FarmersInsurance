# Does Hiring an Attorney Make Expert Communications Privileged?

**Case:** *Yibiao Lu and Jie Hu v. Fire Insurance Exchange* — Court File No. 27-CV-26-11606
**Prepared:** September 19, 2026
**Question asked:** if we hire an attorney and have the attorney communicate with the expert, does that make the communications confidential and non-discoverable?

> **Not legal advice.** I am not a lawyer. This is research for discussion with a Minnesota-licensed attorney, who should confirm all of it before you act on it.
>
> **Verification note (Order ¶13).** Rule text pulled verbatim from revisor.mn.gov on **September 19, 2026**. §7 flags a widely-circulated secondary source that states the opposite of the rule text — do not rely on it.

---

## 1. Short answer

**No — not in Minnesota state court, and not for a testifying expert.**

Hiring an attorney creates a privilege between **you and the attorney**. It does not create one between **the attorney and your expert**. Routing expert communications through counsel does not cloak them, because the protection that would do that job in federal court has no counterpart in the Minnesota rules.

There *are* two structures that genuinely protect expert-related material in Minnesota. Neither depends on hiring a lawyer, and both are available to you now:

- **Retain a separate, non-testifying consulting expert** — Minn. R. Civ. P. 26.02(e)(2), the "exceptional circumstances" shield (§4).
- **Stipulate to the protection with Farmers** under Minn. R. Civ. P. 29 — and there is already a pending letter to Kennedy that is the natural vehicle (§5).

---

## 2. Three different protections — and why none of them reaches a testifying expert

People use "privileged" loosely for three distinct doctrines. They do different work.

### (a) Attorney–client privilege — protects you and your lawyer, not your lawyer and your expert

The privilege covers confidential communications between lawyer and client for the purpose of legal advice. **An expert witness is a third party, not the client.** A letter from your attorney to your engineer is not an attorney–client communication, and disclosing your theories to a third party is the classic way to fall outside the privilege rather than inside it.

There is a narrow exception in some jurisdictions for an expert retained purely to help the lawyer understand technical material (the *Kovel* line, from tax and accounting practice). That is not the situation where the expert is being lined up to testify.

### (b) Work product — Minn. R. Civ. P. 26.02(d), and it is narrower in Minnesota than you would expect

Rule 26.02(d) begins with a phrase that decides this whole question:

> "**Subject to the provisions of Rule 26.02(e)** a party may obtain discovery of documents and tangible things otherwise discoverable pursuant to Rule 26.02(b) and prepared in anticipation of litigation or for trial by or for another party or by or for that other party's representative (**including the other party's attorney, consultant, surety, indemnitor, insurer, or agent**) only upon a showing that the party seeking discovery has substantial need of the materials … and … is unable without undue hardship to obtain the substantial equivalent …"

Two things follow.

1. **"Subject to the provisions of Rule 26.02(e)"** means that where expert discovery is concerned, Rule 26.02(e) governs. Work product does not override it.
2. Minnesota's work-product doctrine is **two-tiered and narrower than the federal single graduated doctrine**. The Minnesota Supreme Court treats true work product as an attorney's *mental impressions, trial strategy, and legal theories* — which get near-absolute protection under the rule's second sentence. Materials prepared in anticipation of litigation that do **not** contain those elements are merely "trial preparation materials," discoverable on a showing of substantial need and undue hardship.

So a lawyer's strategy memo may be inner-tier — right up until the expert reads it and it becomes part of what the expert considered. See (c).

### (c) Rule 26.02(e) — the expert rule, and the gap at the centre of it

> "Discovery of facts known and opinions held by experts … acquired or developed in anticipation of litigation or for trial, may be obtained **only as follows**:
>
> (1)(A) A party may through interrogatories require any other party to identify each person whom the other party expects to call as an expert witness at trial, to state the subject matter on which the expert is expected to testify, and to state the substance of the facts and opinions to which the expert is expected to testify and a summary of the grounds for each opinion.
> (B) **Upon motion**, the court may order further discovery by other means, subject to such restrictions as to scope and such provisions … concerning fees and expenses, as the court may deem appropriate."

**Minnesota Rule 26.02 contains no provision protecting draft expert reports, and none protecting communications between a party's attorney and a testifying expert.** I searched the rule text directly for both. There is no equivalent of **Fed. R. Civ. P. 26(b)(4)(B)** (drafts of expert reports protected as work product regardless of form) or **26(b)(4)(C)** (attorney–expert communications protected, with three narrow exceptions).

This is not a drafting oversight that courts have quietly fixed. A Minnesota practitioner treatment of the work-product doctrine puts it plainly: Minnesota lacks counterparts to those federal rules, whether the work-product doctrine does that job on its own is **unresolved**, and practitioners should **assume draft reports and attorney correspondence lack protection unless a court rules otherwise.**

### (d) And the disclosure obligation runs the other way

Even setting discovery aside, Rule 26.01(b)(2) requires the retained expert's signed report to contain:

> "(B) the **facts or data considered by the witness** in forming them"

"Considered," not "relied on." Material your attorney sends the expert, which the expert reads and takes into account, is arguably within (B) and gets disclosed by **you**, in the report due **3/15/2027** — no motion by Farmers required.

> **A subtle and telling point.** Minnesota adopted the modern federal *disclosure* language in Rule 26.01(b) — including the signed written report and the "facts or data" formulation. It did **not** adopt the accompanying federal *protections* in Rule 26(b)(4)(B)–(C). Minnesota took the half of the 2010 federal package that expands disclosure and left the half that shields the lawyer–expert relationship. That asymmetry is the whole answer to your question.

## 3. What this means in practice

Assume, for your **testifying** expert, that all of the following are discoverable or disclosable:

- every email to and from the expert, including your attorney's;
- every draft report and every marked-up version;
- notes of calls, if they exist;
- anything your attorney sent the expert that the expert considered;
- the expert's own file.

The practical brake is procedural rather than substantive: Rule 26.02(e) says expert discovery may be had "**only as follows**," and anything beyond the identification interrogatory requires a **motion**. Farmers cannot simply notice the deposition or serve a document request on your expert; it has to ask the Court, and the Court sets the scope. That is real friction, and it cuts both ways — it is also why you cannot depose *their* expert without leave, which is the subject of the pending stipulation.

But friction is not a privilege. A judge can order it produced, and the standard for doing so is discretionary, not the "exceptional circumstances" standard in §4.

**Working rule, unchanged from the criteria memo:** write every instruction to the expert as though it will be read aloud to the jury. Give facts and documents, ask questions, and do not suggest conclusions. An expert whose file shows they were handed a conclusion is an expert whose foundational reliability under Rule 702 is already in trouble — the same attack the repository plans to run against Prieve.

## 4. What actually works, part one — the non-testifying consulting expert

This is the real shield, and it is in the same rule:

> "**(2)** A party may discover facts known or opinions held by an expert who has been retained or specially employed by another party in anticipation of litigation or preparation for trial and **who is not expected to be called as a witness at trial**, only as provided in Rule 35.02 or **upon a showing of exceptional circumstances under which it is impracticable for the party seeking discovery to obtain facts or opinions on the same subject by other means**."

"Exceptional circumstances" is a demanding standard, and Rule 26.02(e)(3) adds mandatory fee-shifting against whoever seeks that discovery. A consulting expert is genuinely protected in a way a testifying expert is not.

**The structure this implies — two separate retentions, documented as such from day one:**

| | Consulting expert | Testifying expert |
|---|---|---|
| Role | Evaluates the case candidly; tells you the weaknesses; helps you frame discovery | Forms and defends the opinions at trial |
| Protection | Rule 26.02(e)(2) — exceptional circumstances | **None** — assume full exposure |
| Communications | Can be frank | Facts and documents only |
| Will testify? | **No — and this must be true, not just stated** | Yes |

Two cautions, both important:

- **The designation has to be real.** If the consulting expert is later designated to testify, the protection is gone. Decide the role at retention and do not swap.
- **This costs money.** Two retentions on a residential window claim is a meaningful expense. A cheaper version: use the **first conversation** with each candidate — before any retention — to get the candid read, since you are not obliged to retain anyone you talk to. The §7 pre-retention packet in the V2 shortlist is built for exactly that.

There is also a sequencing point specific to this case that cuts the other way. `Expert_Discovery_and_Challenge_Research_2026-09-17.md` §1.4 notes that **Prieve was retained in the ordinary course of adjusting the claim, not in anticipation of litigation** — which is why his file is reachable under Rules 26.02(b) and 34 rather than shielded as a consulting expert. Do not build a structure for yourselves that hands Farmers the mirror-image argument about your own consultant.

## 5. What actually works, part two — stipulate for it

Minnesota has no draft-report protection **by rule**. The parties can create one **by agreement**. Minn. R. Civ. P. 29 permits the parties by stipulation to "modify other procedures governing or **limitations placed upon discovery**," subject only to an exception for extensions of Rule 33/34/36 response time that would interfere with set deadlines.

**There is already a letter pending that is the natural vehicle.** `../Expert_Discovery_Stipulation_Letter_to_Kennedy_2026-09-12.md` proposes four terms on expert depositions and relies on Rule 29 as the authority. A **fifth term** fits the same instrument:

> **Proposed term 5 — Draft reports and expert communications.** Drafts of any expert report or disclosure, in any form, and communications between a party (or its counsel) and any testifying expert retained by that party, are not discoverable, except to the extent they (a) relate to the expert's compensation, (b) identify facts or data the party or its counsel provided and the expert considered, or (c) identify assumptions the party or its counsel provided and the expert relied upon. This term applies equally to both parties.

That wording tracks the three exceptions in Fed. R. Civ. P. 26(b)(4)(C), which keeps it familiar to opposing counsel and keeps the genuinely important things — compensation, facts supplied, assumptions supplied — on the table.

**Why Farmers should want it, and this is the argument to make:** the stipulation is mutual, and on the current record Farmers has more to protect than you do. Farmers will disclose a retained causation expert on **4/17/2027** who will have exchanged drafts with Kennedy Law Firm. Your existing letter already makes this "why Defendant should want this" argument about depositions; term 5 rides the same logic.

**If Farmers refuses term 5, that is itself informative** — and you will have flagged, in writing and seven months early, that both sides are operating without draft protection.

## 6. So should you hire an attorney? — a straight answer on the tradeoffs

The premise of the question is wrong, but the underlying instinct is not. Counsel would help this case in several concrete ways. It would also cost you two things the repository has identified as genuine pro se advantages.

**What counsel buys:**

- **Attorney–client privilege over your own communications** — which, as `../../../LegalReferences/discoveryOverview.md` notes, a self-represented party does not have at all. That is a real gap today, distinct from the expert question.
- **Work product over counsel's own analysis** (inner tier: mental impressions, strategy, legal theories).
- Someone to receive an **attorneys'-eyes-only** designation if Farmers reverses its 9/14/2026 position — the largest unaddressed exposure identified in `../../../OfficialCourtDocuments/SchedulingOrder_Impact_Analysis_2026-09-10.md` §3(c). Today there is no one on your side who could receive such a designation.
- Capacity for the compressed **§ 604.18** window (3/15/2027 → 4/23/2027, about 5½ weeks) that the same memo calls unrealistic to do pro se.
- Hearing dates, motion practice, and the Rule 702 challenge.

**What counsel costs, beyond fees:**

- **Order ¶9:** "Parties represented by an attorney may not reach out to the Court directly." Your direct line to the assigned clerk closes.
- **Order ¶12(a):** attorneys must use eFS. You are currently excluded from that mandate.

Neither is decisive. Both are real, and neither has anything to do with expert privilege — which is the point: hire counsel for the reasons above, not on the theory that it will seal the expert file.

## 7. ⚠️ A secondary source that states the opposite — do not rely on it

A widely-circulated page on Minnesota expert-witness discovery (expertinstitute.com) states that Minnesota "does not typically mandate formal written expert reports," and that draft reports and attorney–expert communications are protected, "reinforced through 2018 rule amendments aligning Minnesota with federal standards."

**Both statements are contradicted by the rule text.** Rule 26.01(b)(2) expressly requires that the disclosure "must be accompanied by a written report — prepared and signed by the witness" for a retained expert. And no provision anywhere in Rule 26 protects drafts or attorney–expert communications. The same page also cites the consulting-expert shield to Rule 26.02(d); it is Rule 26.02(e)(2).

Rule 26 does carry an advisory committee comment dated 2018, which is probably the seed of the error — but what Minnesota adopted from the federal package was the **disclosure** side, not the **protection** side. This is a good example of why Order ¶13 exists.

---

## 8. Recommendation

1. **Do not restructure expert communications in the belief that counsel makes them privileged.** In Minnesota state court it does not.
2. **Assume everything sent to or from the testifying expert is discoverable.** Instructions in writing, factual, no suggested conclusions.
3. **Add term 5 to the pending expert-discovery stipulation** and send it. It is free, it is mutual, it is the only route to federal-style draft protection in this case, and a refusal is worth knowing about now.
4. **Use the pre-retention conversations** with expert candidates for the candid assessment, before any retention exists to be discovered.
5. **Consider a separate consulting expert** under Rule 26.02(e)(2) only if the budget supports two retentions — and if so, document the non-testifying role at the outset and never convert it.
6. **Evaluate hiring counsel on its own merits** — privilege over your own communications, the AEO exposure, the § 604.18 window — weighed against losing direct access to the clerk under ¶9.

## 1. The criticism is correct — here is what went wrong

V2 and V3 kept surfacing building-envelope and structural firms (WJE, Inspec, Encompass, Read Consulting). That happened because I was searching on **credentials that get published** — AAMA accreditation, fenestration certifications, fractography protocols — and those belong to building-science firms.

The hail world does not advertise that way. So this round I searched the way the hail industry is actually organised, and the result is more useful than another list of names.

**The finding that matters: not one of these is an engineering firm.** Where a company is listed it is a roofing contractor. **HCI is a contractor and adjuster credential, not a professional engineering licence.** These people can competently observe and document hail damage. They cannot give the Rule 702 engineering causation opinion your report needs on 3/15/2027 — a Haag-certified roofer opining on impact energy to an extruded-aluminium window assembly is exactly the witness Farmers moves to exclude.

**What they are genuinely useful for:** a fast, cheap, local second set of eyes on the windows before you commit to an engineer, and as a potential fact witness on observed damage. Not as your testifying causation expert.

## 5. Why a policyholder-side hail engineer is genuinely hard to find — and the route that works

The structure of this market, as the searches actually revealed it:

- **Local hail work** is done by roofing contractors and public adjusters (§2).
- **Engineering causation work** is done by large national firms whose stated clients are insurers and claims professionals (§3).
- **Minnesota engineering firms** with forensic practices exist (§4) but are general building-science firms that also do storm work — and most take carrier work.

An independent, PE-credentialed, policyholder-available hail engineer is a genuinely scarce commodity. That is not a research failure; it is the market. And it is exactly why the August memo's §5 said the single best source is **a referral from a Minnesota policyholder-side attorney** — they keep short lists of engineers who hold up under cross-examination, because they retain them repeatedly.

So the highest-value calls this week are not to engineers. They are to the people who fight hail denials in Hennepin County every week.

## 6. The script for the referral calls

Keep it to two minutes. You are not hiring them.

> "I'm a homeowner in Eden Prairie, self-represented in a hail case in Hennepin County against Farmers. It's past appraisal and in litigation, and my expert report is due in March. I'm not calling to retain counsel today — I'm trying to find a forensic engineer who does hail causation on **windows**, not roofs, and who will work for a homeowner. Is there anyone you'd point me to?"

Ask all three. If two of them name the same engineer, that is your answer — and it is better information than any directory produced in this entire file.

**Also ask each:** "Have you dealt with Prieve Engineering or Kennedy Law Firm?" Their answer is free intelligence about your opponents.

## 8. Honest assessment

You may not find a Twin Cities engineer whose specialty is *hail damage to windows*. That specialty barely exists as a standalone practice anywhere — the V3 research found the published expertise concentrated in Australian academics and a handful of fenestration consultants scattered across Florida and South Carolina.

The realistic best outcome is a **local, licensed PE with genuine hail-causation experience who is willing to apply it to window assemblies**, supported by Encompass's lab testing of the actual units. That is a person AET or a Livgard & Lloyd referral can plausibly produce. It is not a person a directory search will find, which is why §6 is the most important section here.

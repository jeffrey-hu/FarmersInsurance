## Bottom line

The draft is well-organized, correctly framed, and cites the right rules. It is **not ready to serve** — but the fixes are additions and clarifications, not a rewrite of the theory.

**Claiming replacement cost is right.** The policy's repair-first language defers the replacement-cost payment; it does not forfeit it. ## Part I — Rule compliance

The governing text, verbatim (Minn. R. Civ. P. 26.01(a)(1)):

- **(A)** "the name and, if known, the address and telephone number of each individual likely to have discoverable information — along with the subjects of that information — that the disclosing party may use to support its claims or defenses, unless the use would be solely for impeachment"
- **(B)** "a copy — or a description by category and location — of all documents, electronically stored information, and tangible things that the disclosing party has in its possession, custody, or control and may use to support its claims or defenses…"
- **(C)** "a computation of each category of damages claimed by the disclosing party — who must also make available for inspection and copying as under Rule 34 the documents or other evidentiary material… on which each computation is based, including materials bearing on the nature and extent of injuries suffered"
- **(D)** "…any insurance agreement under which an insurance business may be liable to satisfy all or part of a possible judgment in the action…"

And the provision that defeats most "we'll supplement later" entries — **Rule 26.01(a)(5)**: "A party is not excused from making its disclosures because it has not fully investigated the case."

## Part III — The damages computation

This is where the work is.

### $-1. Claim replacement cost — but say why, and disclose ACV in the alternative

> **Correction (revised September 6, 2026).** An earlier version of this review advised making actual cash value the *primary* computation and demoting RCV to a contingent holdback. **That advice was wrong and has been withdrawn.** It read the policy's repair-first language as a cap on entitlement when it is a *payment-sequencing* mechanic, and it would have conceded a limitation Farmers has not pleaded. The corrected analysis follows.

**The two provisions, read carefully:**

> **Coverage A loss settlement:** "When the cost to repair or replace damaged property is more than $2,500, we will pay no more than the actual cash value of the loss **until** actual repair or replacement is completed."

> **Section I – Property Conditions, ¶ 6 (Replacement cost settlement):** "For any loss to property which may be settled on a replacement cost basis, you have 12 months **from the date of our first payment toward actual cash value** to make a claim for any additional amounts due for replacement cost settlement. The property must be actually repaired or replaced to make a claim for replacement cost."

Neither provision forfeits the replacement-cost benefit. The first defers it — "no more than… **until**" — and the second sets a window for claiming it. Both are built on an assumed sequence: the insurer pays ACV, the insured repairs, the insured claims the holdback.

**Farmers never took step one.** It paid **$0**. The 12-month window is expressly measured "from the date of our first payment toward actual cash value," and no such payment was ever made. **The clock the condition depends on never started.** That is a textual argument, not an equitable one, and it is the strongest form of the point: Plaintiffs are not asking to be excused from a condition — they are pointing out that the condition's trigger never occurred.

**The equitable argument backs it up.** Where an insurer denies liability and pays nothing, courts widely hold the insured is not confined to ACV, on the prevention-of-performance principle that a party may not rely on the non-occurrence of a condition it caused. See *Rockford Mutual Insurance Co. v. Pirtle* (Ind. Ct. App. 2009) (insurer's failure to advance funds may excuse compliance); *Ferguson v. Lakeland Mutual Insurance Co.* (Pa. Super. Ct. 1991) (repair requirement unconscionable where insurer denied liability); *Ward v. Merrimack Mutual Fire Insurance Co.* (N.J. Super. Ct.); *Utica Mutual Insurance Co. v. Cincinnati Insurance Co.*, 2019 WL 290162 (E.D. Pa. 2019). Minnesota recognizes the underlying principle in general contract law: "A party who has entered into an obligation to pay will not be permitted to set up his own voluntary default to defeat the same." *Dana v. St. Paul Investment Co.*, 42 Minn. 194, 44 N.W. 55 (1889), quoted in *Space Center, Inc. v. 451 Corp.*, 298 N.W.2d 443 (Minn. 1980).

Notably, the recognized **limit** on that doctrine does not reach this case: the condition is generally *not* excused where the insurer admitted liability and paid ACV, leaving only a valuation dispute. Farmers admitted nothing and paid nothing.

**Be honest about the risk.** There is a real split. *Buckley Towers Condominium, Inc. v. QBE Insurance Corp.* (11th Cir. 2010) rejected the prevention doctrine, reasoning that non-payment creates only "cost and inconvenience"; *Kahlig Enterprises, Inc. v. Affiliated FM Insurance Co.* (5th Cir. 2024) is the recent insurer-side authority. And **I found no controlling Minnesota decision squarely on this question** — the argument rests on the policy's own text plus general Minnesota contract principles. Do not treat it as settled; do build the record for it.

**The record to build now.** Insurer-side counsel will argue that Plaintiffs must show they *would have* repaired but for the denial. Two reasons support not having repaired, and the order matters:

1. **Financial** — Farmers paid nothing, and a $300,000-plus replacement cannot be self-funded. This is the reason that satisfies the prevention doctrine.
2. **Evidentiary** — replacing the windows now destroys the physical evidence of the very damage Farmers denies exists.

Lead with the first. Standing alone, the second invites the argument that Plaintiffs would not have repaired regardless — which is precisely the showing that defeats prevention. Stated together, in that order, they are consistent: *we would have repaired had Farmers paid; we have not repaired because Farmers paid nothing, and because repairing now would destroy the evidence.*

Consider also proposing an inspection-and-preservation protocol (joint expert inspection, retention of removed sashes and components, documented metrology) so the evidentiary reason does not harden into an indefinite bar to repair.

**What this means for Section C — the revised fix:**

1. **Keep RCV as the primary computation.** $303,820.78 stays where it is.
2. **Add one sentence of basis** explaining why: no payment toward ACV was ever made, so the policy's 12-month replacement-cost window has not begun to run, and Defendant's denial and non-payment prevented repair.
3. **Add ACV as an alternative computation.** This is the part of the original advice that survives, and it matters for a different reason than I first gave: Rule 26.01(a)(1)(C) requires a computation of "each category of damages **claimed**," the appraisal already put ACV figures in the case ($12,800 RCV / $6,400 ACV), and under *Wilcox v. State Farm Fire & Casualty Co.*, 874 N.W.2d 780 (Minn. 2016) actual cash value is determined by the broad evidence rule with depreciation a fact question. If this case is tried or settled on an ACV measure, an undisclosed ACV number is exposed to Rule 37.03(a). Disclose it **in the alternative**, expressly without conceding that recovery is limited to it.
4. If ACV cannot be computed yet, say so, state the method, and commit to a Rule 26.05 supplementation date. The award's own **50% ACV/RCV ratio** is a defensible interim placeholder drawn from Defendant's document — far stronger than "to be determined."

### $-1A. The 10% overhead-and-profit line needs support

$27,620.07 of the $303,820.78 is a flat 10% overhead-and-profit markup. In **Great Northwest Insurance Co. v. Campbell** (Minn. July 30, 2025), the Minnesota Supreme Court held that the insured had not shown overhead and profit were recoverable, because he failed to establish they were a cost *required by code* within Minn. Stat. § 65A.10, subd. 1.

**Read that holding narrowly and accurately.** Campbell concerned the statutory ordinance-and-law obligation, not a policy's general replacement-cost promise, and the insured there had already completed the repairs. It does **not** hold that O&P is never recoverable in a Minnesota property claim. But it does show this Court's supreme court will not simply assume O&P.

**Practical effect:** be ready to prove that a repair of this scope and complexity — thirty-plus window units across multiple elevations, requiring an on-site lift, interior casing, and trade coordination — reasonably requires a general contractor, which is the usual predicate for O&P. Note the $3,404.70 lift line and the $51,253.02 installation-labor line as support. This is not a reason to drop the markup; it is a reason to know why it is there before Farmers asks.

### $-2. No deductible line and no award credit — in a case that is entirely deductible arithmetic

The whole dispute is this calculation:

| | |
|---|---:|
| Appraisal award, ACV | $6,400 |
| Windstorm/hail deductible (2% × $886,000 Coverage A) | $(17,720) |
| **Net paid to date** | **$0** |

*(Verified: 2% of $886,000 = $17,720; award ACV falls $11,320 short.)*

Section C shows gross figures with no deductible line and no credit for the award, mentioning the deductible only in a trailing sentence attached to the proof of loss. Because Plaintiffs recover a dollar only by proving covered loss **above $17,720**, the computation should carry the arithmetic on its face:

> Covered loss − $17,720 deductible − $0 paid to date = net contract damages.

Show it once at RCV and once at ACV, consistent with the alternative computations in § $-1.

Doing this arithmetic yourself is more credible than making Farmers do it, and it demonstrates that Plaintiffs understand exactly what they must prove.

### $-3. The bridge from "damaged windows" to "every window" is never stated

The $303,820.78 covers **32 window line items totaling 38 units** (plus two sliding-door line items) — effectively the whole house. But the spreadsheet's own "Dents in Report" column marks several units "???" (no documented dent), Prieve documented damage at **at least twenty-five** windows, and the panel awarded **two**. Note also that the category is labeled "Window replacement" while the estimate includes two sliding doors; either relabel the category or break the doors out.

There is a real legal theory supporting whole-house replacement — matching. **Cedar Bluff Townhome Condominium Ass'n v. American Family Mutual Insurance Co., 857 N.W.2d 290 (Minn. 2014)**, held that "comparable material and quality" requires a reasonable match between new and existing material, and entitled the insured to replacement beyond the damaged units. The disclosure never mentions it. As written, the number reads as a contractor's whole-house quote stapled to a hail claim.

A second Minnesota authority helps here and is worth pairing with Cedar Bluff: **Great Northwest Insurance Co. v. Campbell** (Minn. July 30, 2025) held that where building-code compliance requires replacing **undamaged** components in order to replace the damaged ones, Minn. Stat. § 65A.10, subd. 1 requires the insurer to cover that cost, and policy language excluding it is invalid. If replacing the damaged window units triggers current code obligations touching adjacent or undamaged units, that is a statutory route to a broader scope that runs alongside the common-law matching argument.

**Fix:** add one sentence of theory to the Basis cell — that full replacement is required to achieve a reasonable match, the original Semco units being unavailable — and preserve the § 65A.10 code-compliance ground.

**But know the wrinkle before Farmers raises it.** This policy's language is not Cedar Bluff's. It pays the replacement cost of "**that part of the dwelling… damaged** for equivalent construction with materials of **like kind and quality**." Farmers will press "that part… damaged" as a textual limit and argue "like kind and quality" is a looser standard than the "comparable material and quality" Cedar Bluff construed. The argument is available and worth making; it is not automatic, and it should not be assumed in a document certified under Rule 26.07.

### $-4. The strongest part of the claim has no damages category at all

Section C has no line for the **non-window exterior components**: dented downspouts (Prieve A-42, A-91–A-93, A-98, A-99), flashing (A-103, A-128, A-129), trim board (A-53, A-90, A-148), and the sliding-door screen (A-141).

Leaving that out of the damages computation is the single most consequential omission in Section C. **Add the category** — with a contractor number if one can be obtained in 19 days, or with a stated method and a Rule 26.05 supplementation date if not.

## Part IV — Strategy

### E-1. Splitting the adverse-witness row does more than satisfy the rule

Listing Farmers' personnel as adverse is right — Rule 26.01(a)(1)(A) excludes only witnesses used *solely* for impeachment, and these five are substantive. But five names behind one generic subject line reads as a placeholder. Five rows with five specific subjects reads as a developed theory, and it lays the groundwork for the deposition notices already drafted.

### E-2. Keep Foster's subject line neutral

The draft says Foster will testify about "the appraisal process and **the panel's hail-causation finding**." That is Plaintiffs' *Quade* position, and it is fine — but Count III's final alternative seeks vacatur partly on the ground that Foster was selected by Pure Construction rather than by Plaintiffs. Alternative positions are permitted, but do not hand Farmers a sentence in which Plaintiffs endorse Foster's finding while attacking his selection.

**Suggested subject:** "The conduct of the § 65A.01 appraisal; his engagement, selection, and compensation; his file and workpapers; and the basis for the September 12, 2025 award."

### E-3. Keep legal characterization out of the disclosure

Section A and Section C carry some argument ("the panel's hail-causation finding," "comparable current-production equivalents"). None of it is harmful — it is all already in the Complaint — but disclosures work best as flat factual inventories. Save the framing for briefs, where Farmers cannot quote it back as a binding characterization.

### E-4. Serving a clean, complete disclosure on time is worth more here than usual

Farmers pleaded only three boilerplate affirmative defenses and — notably — **no limitations defense**. Its Answer is thin. A pro se plaintiff who serves a precise, fully sourced, on-time disclosure establishes a posture with the Court that will matter when the contested discovery arrives: Brau's missing notes, the full 2020 Xactimate and photo log, and the umpire's file. Those fights are coming, and credibility built now is spent then.

### E-5. Calendar the Rule 26.05 supplementation triggers on the day of service

Rule 26.05 imposes a continuing duty. Put these on a calendar now, each with a date:

- Retention of the forensic engineering and meteorology experts → supplement Section A
- ACV computation once depreciation is analyzed → supplement Section C
- Neighbor identities and addresses → supplement Section A
- Contractor numbers for the non-window components, siding, and water/mold remediation → supplement Section C
- Any documents received from Farmers' production that Plaintiffs will use affirmatively → supplement Section B

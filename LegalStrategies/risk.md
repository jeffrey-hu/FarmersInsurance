## PART 1 — Plaintiffs' Near-Term Out-of-Pocket Costs

### 1.1 What this part addresses

A claim that Plaintiffs prevail on every count of v2 produces uncapped MCFA fee-shifting under Minn. Stat. § 8.31, Subd. 3a / § 325F.70 and § 604.18 fee-shifting up to $100,000 plus taxable costs up to $250,000. Looking only at those numbers, a reader might conclude the family will not pay litigation costs out of pocket. **That conclusion is wrong.** Six structural facts make Plaintiffs' near-term out-of-pocket cost real even on optimistic outcome assumptions.

### 1.2 The two fee-shifting statutes — what they do and what they don't do

**MCFA fee-shifting (Count V in v2).** If Plaintiffs prevail on the MCFA count, the court "shall" award reasonable attorney fees, costs, and costs of investigation under Minn. Stat. § 8.31, Subd. 3a and § 325F.70. There is no statutory cap.

**§ 604.18 fee-shifting (post-filing motion under Subd. 4(a)).** If Plaintiffs prevail on a § 604.18 motion, the court awards "taxable costs" equal to one-half of proceeds awarded in excess of Defendant's pre-trial offer (capped at $250,000) plus reasonable attorney fees actually incurred (capped at $100,000) plus interest, costs, and disbursements.

Both look powerful on paper. Six structural facts limit them in practice.

### 1.3 Six reasons near-term out-of-pocket cost is real

#### 1.3.1 Fee-shifting is contingent on prevailing — losing means no shift

Both statutes shift fees only if Plaintiffs win on the relevant count. If C3 falls out at summary judgment on the "in connection with the sale" hurdle (the principal interpretive risk flagged at ~30/100 in `C3.md`), there is no MCFA fee award. If the § 604.18 motion is denied at the prima-facie stage or if Subd. 4(b)'s appraisal bar disposes of the remedy, there is no § 604.18 award. In a worst-case scenario where both fee-shifting routes fail and the contract claim recovers below the deductible, **Plaintiffs receive no fee-shifting and bear all litigation costs themselves.**

#### 1.3.2 Fee-shifting is paid at the end — not as expenses are incurred

Even if Plaintiffs ultimately win and a fee award is entered, that happens at the conclusion of the case — typically twelve to thirty-six months after the complaint is filed. In the meantime, every month's legal bill, expert invoice, court reporter fee, and filing receipt must be paid in real money. Counsel does not work for free against a future fee award; experts do not work for free; the court does not waive filing fees. The cash-flow profile is: pay continuously for the duration of the case → maybe recover at the end. Fee-shifting is a reimbursement, not a financing mechanism.

#### 1.3.3 Fee-shifting does not cover all litigation expenses

Even if both fee-shifting routes succeed, several categories of cost are not fully recoverable:

- **§ 604.18 caps attorney fees at $100,000.** First-party hail-claim litigation through summary judgment and trial commonly runs $80,000–$250,000 in attorney fees alone. Anything above the $100,000 cap is the family's expense.
- **Expert witness fees are partially recoverable as costs but not unlimited.** A first-party hail case at trial typically requires (a) a hail-damage forensic engineer to rebut Prieve, (b) a window-matching expert to support the *Cedar Bluff* / J6944 theory, (c) a replacement-cost / valuation expert, and possibly (d) an appraisal-process expert to address the *Mork* / *Herll* sub-claims under Count III. Total expert budget alone can run $20,000–$60,000.
- **Filing fees, service-of-process fees, court reporter fees, mediation fees, trial-exhibit preparation, deposition transcripts, and travel** are recoverable as "disbursements" under Minn. Stat. § 549.04 only if Plaintiffs prevail and only at statutory rates that often do not match actual cost.
- **Retainer requirements.** Most Minnesota first-party bad-faith firms require a retainer up front before starting work. Retainers in this category routinely run $5,000–$25,000.

#### 1.3.4 Contingency representation may not be available — this case has direct evidence of that

The June 11, 2025 refund of the family's $30 Minnesota Lawyer Referral Service fee, with the explanation that "we do not have an attorney available to pursue your claim through the referral service," is direct evidence that contingency-fee counsel was unavailable for this case at that point. Several factors explain it:

- The deductible math ($17,720 deductible against a $12,800 RCV award) means the most likely recovery scenario does not break the deductible without scope expansion. Contingency lawyers calculate expected recovery × probability and decline cases where the math does not work.
- The procedural complexity (post-appraisal posture; *Mork* / *Herll* / *Quade* interplay; § 604.18 appraisal-bar question) makes the case harder to handicap than a typical contingent fee matter.
- The pro-se posture going in raises diligence concerns for prospective contingency counsel.

If contingency counsel is eventually retained, the typical first-party bad-faith contingency fee runs 33% to 40% of recovery, plus the family advancing costs. So a $200,000 recovery becomes ~$120,000 to the family before costs and ~$110,000 after. The family is also typically obligated to advance expert fees and disbursements during the case.

If hourly counsel is retained, the family pays as work is done — typically $25,000 to $75,000 over the first year of litigation, with continuing costs through trial.

#### 1.3.5 § 604.18 cannot reduce near-term cost because it is a post-pleading motion

§ 604.18 is added by Subd. 4(a) motion *after* discovery has developed the bad-faith record. The earliest realistic timing for the Subd. 4(a) motion is six to twelve months into the case. Any fee-shifting under § 604.18 is awarded only at the end of the case, after the Subd. 4(a) prima facie ruling has come back favorably *and* after Plaintiffs have prevailed on the merits. Until then, no § 604.18 fees flow to anyone.

#### 1.3.6 The Subd. 4(b) appraisal bar threatens § 604.18 entirely

The most important caveat. Minn. Stat. § 604.18, Subd. 4(b) provides that "an award of taxable costs under this section is not available in any claim that is resolved or confirmed by … appraisal." Whether the September 12, 2025 appraisal award "resolves" the claim within Subd. 4(b)'s meaning is an open Minnesota question — and the Subd. 4(b) bar is the principal procedural threat to the § 604.18 fee-shifting remedy in this case. If a court reads Subd. 4(b) broadly, the § 604.18 fee-shifting remedy disappears entirely, and only the C3 path remains.

### 1.4 Realistic litigation budget through trial

| Category | Low end | High end |
|---|---|---|
| Attorney fees (hourly) | $60,000 | $200,000 |
| Filing, service, court reporter, mediation | $5,000 | $15,000 |
| Expert witness fees | $20,000 | $60,000 |
| Trial exhibits and disbursements | $5,000 | $20,000 |
| **Total Plaintiffs' out-of-pocket through trial** | **~$90,000** | **~$295,000** |

Of that, the recoverable portion if Plaintiffs prevail on C3 and the § 604.18 motion (best case) might run $100,000–$250,000. The non-recoverable portion (worst case where neither succeeds) is the entire amount.

For the family specifically, the meaningful number is the **money they must front before any reimbursement.** Even on the most favorable assumptions about the eventual fee award, that front-loaded number is in the tens of thousands of dollars at minimum, and into six figures if the case goes to trial without contingency representation.

### 1.5 Why this matters for the strategic decisions in `bowl.md`

The "near-term out-of-pocket cost is real" warning is the practical reality check behind several § 0 strategic decisions:

- It is part of why `00_Overview_Complaint_Drafts.md` favors **v2 Balanced** rather than **v3 Comprehensive** — v3 adds Rule 12 surface area without proportional fee-shifting upside.
- It is part of why **retaining experienced bad-faith counsel** is a Critical item — without counsel, the family bears 100% of the work in addition to 100% of the disbursement cost, and the chance of capturing fee-shifting at the end drops substantially.
- It is part of why **issuing a Subd. 1(b) offer of settlement under § 549.09 contemporaneous with filing** matters — locking in the offer-of-settlement posture early protects the prejudgment-interest accrual that does not require fee-shifting and runs by operation of law.
- It is part of why **C3 is included in v2 even though it is rated 20–30/100 standalone** — uncapped C3 fee-shifting is the only protection the family has against the § 604.18 cap and the Subd. 4(b) appraisal-bar risk.

The honest framing: even on a "reasonable" outcome where Plaintiffs prevail on most counts and recover meaningfully, the family will likely incur tens of thousands of dollars in real money before any fee-shifting reimbursement arrives.

## PART 2 — Defendant's Potential Cost Recovery if Plaintiffs Lose

### 2.1 What this part addresses

If Plaintiffs lose — in whole or on individual counts — Defendant Fire Insurance Exchange can recover certain costs from Plaintiffs. Minnesota follows the American Rule (each party pays its own attorney fees), so Defendant's exposure is significantly less than Plaintiffs' own litigation costs. But it is still real money the family can lose. Three categories define Defendant's exposure.

### 2.2 The legal framework — three categories of Defendant cost recovery

#### 2.2.1 Routine taxable costs and disbursements — Minn. Stat. §§ 549.02 and 549.04 plus Minn. R. Civ. P. 54.04

These are awarded "as a matter of course to the prevailing party" subject to the court's discretion. They cover the actual out-of-pocket expenses Defendant incurred prosecuting its defense.

**§ 549.02 statutory costs** are fixed by statute at modest amounts — $200 for district court actions, plus a small additional amount when the case proceeds to trial.

**§ 549.04 disbursements** are actual reasonable out-of-pocket expenses, generally including:

- Filing fees on Defendant's motions
- Service-of-process fees on subpoenas Defendant serves
- Court reporter fees and deposition transcript costs
- Statutory witness fees ($20/day plus mileage under Minn. Stat. § 357.22 — *not* expert preparation fees)
- Subpoena duces tecum costs
- Copy and exhibit-preparation costs
- Statutory expert witness fees, capped at a "reasonable expert fee" actually paid

**What is NOT included under §§ 549.02 / 549.04:** Defendant's attorney fees, in-house staff time, computerized legal research charges (treatment varies; many courts exclude or limit them), and unrecovered overhead.

#### 2.2.2 Sanctions for frivolous or bad-faith litigation — Minn. Stat. § 549.211 and Minn. R. Civ. P. 11

These are NOT awarded as a matter of course. They require a finding that a specific claim or position was "frivolous, costly to taxpayers, [or] presented for any improper purpose."

If awarded, sanctions can include Defendant's actual attorney fees and costs incurred responding to the frivolous claim. Defendant must move and the court must make findings under Minn. Stat. § 549.211, Subd. 4. This is a discretionary remedy, not automatic.

#### 2.2.3 Minn. R. Civ. P. 68 Offer of Settlement

If Defendant serves a Rule 68 offer that Plaintiffs reject, and Plaintiffs ultimately recover less than the offer, the rule shifts post-offer costs. Minnesota's Rule 68 has been amended several times; the current version provides that a rejecting offeree who does not improve on the offer at trial must pay the offeror's "costs and disbursements" incurred after the offer — but generally NOT the offeror's attorney fees (with limited exceptions). Post-offer disbursements usually run to a few thousand dollars in a typical case but can be larger if the offer is served early.

### 2.3 Concrete dollar estimates for this case

A realistic estimate of Defendant's recoverable taxable costs in a defendant-prevails scenario, based on the procedural posture this case will take:

| Category | Low end | High end | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Statutory costs (§ 549.02) | $200 | $500 | Fixed by statute |
| Filing and motion fees | $200 | $1,000 | Defendant's motion practice (12, summary judgment, in limines) |

The realistic midpoint for a defendant-prevails-on-all-counts scenario is roughly **$8,000 to $15,000** in recoverable taxable costs. That is meaningful but not catastrophic — approximately the scale of one to three months of legal-fee billing.

### 2.4 Where the larger risk lies — sanctions exposure on individual counts

The biggest dollar risk to the family is not routine taxable costs but **§ 549.211 / Rule 11 sanctions** if any individual count is dismissed as frivolous. Sanctions can include Defendant's actual attorney fees on that count. The exposure varies sharply across the three drafts.

#### 2.4.1 v1 (Streamlined) — Sanction risk: very low

A1 (breach of contract), A2 (declaratory judgment), and A3 (clarify/modify the appraisal) are all squarely within Minnesota first-party hail-claim litigation practice. None could plausibly draw a sanctions ruling. **Estimated sanctions exposure: $0.**

#### 2.4.2 v2 (Balanced — Recommended) — Sanction risk: low to modest

Adds B2 (fraud / negligent misrepresentation) and C3 (Minnesota Consumer Fraud Act). Both are non-frivolous as drafted but require careful pleading.

- **B2 risk:** If pled without Rule 9.02 particularity for at least one specific misrepresentation, the count could be dismissed at Rule 12 and Defendant could move for sanctions. The drafted count anchors the claim in Prieve's "no hail in Eden Prairie after 2020" representation, which is concrete and disprovable. Sanctions risk: low if the complaint is filed as drafted; modest if attempted recharacterization arguments collapse the count.
- **C3 risk:** The "in connection with the sale of any merchandise" hurdle is the principal interpretive challenge. Even if a court ultimately disagrees with the *Force* continuing-relationship reading and dismisses the count, that disposition is not "frivolous" — it is the resolution of a contested doctrinal question. Sanctions risk: very low.

**Estimated v2 sanctions exposure: $0 to $15,000**, almost entirely contingent on B2 being pled poorly. A careful pleading drives this exposure toward zero.

#### 2.4.3 v3 (Comprehensive) — Sanction risk: meaningful

Adds A4 (unjust enrichment) and B4 (standalone estoppel/waiver) on top of v2. Both face direct doctrinal headwinds.

- **A4 risk:** *United States Fire Insurance Co. v. Minnesota State Zoological Board*, 307 N.W.2d 490, 497 (Minn. 1981), and *Holman v. CPT Corp.*, 457 N.W.2d 740 (Minn. Ct. App. 1990), squarely hold that unjust enrichment is unavailable where the parties' rights are governed by a valid contract. The policy is plainly valid and in force. Defendant will move to dismiss A4 on the pleadings, and a court is likely to grant it. The drafted A4 count is framed as alternative pleading under Minn. R. Civ. P. 8.05(2), which gives some protection — but in *practice*, repeated alternative pleading of a count clearly foreclosed by Minnesota Supreme Court authority can be characterized as Rule 11-violative if pursued past the inevitable dismissal motion. The unpublished Minnesota appellate decision that sanctioned a plaintiff for pursuing unjust enrichment after the defendant admitted contract liability (cited in `A4.md` § 2.1) is a real warning. Sanctions exposure: **$5,000 to $20,000** if A4 is pursued past the first dismissal opportunity.
- **B4 risk:** Minnesota does not generally recognize waiver/estoppel as standalone causes of action — they are affirmative defenses under Minn. R. Civ. P. 8.03. *Shannon v. Great American Insurance Co.*, 276 N.W.2d 77 (Minn. 1979), limits what estoppel can do for an insured. A Rule 12 dismissal is likely. As long as the count is not re-asserted after dismissal and the family does not litigate the same theory through summary judgment, sanctions risk is low. Sanctions exposure: **$0 to $10,000.**

**Estimated v3 sanctions exposure: $5,000 to $30,000**, principally from A4. This is a real reason `00_Overview_Complaint_Drafts.md` recommends v2 over v3 unless the family is comfortable with that exposure.

### 2.5 Two structural protections the family already has

Two factors meaningfully cap Defendant's overall exposure.

#### 2.5.1 The American Rule controls for the substantive merits

Minnesota's default is each-side-pays-its-own-attorney-fees. Even if Defendant wins on every count, Defendant cannot recover its own defense attorney fees absent a sanctions finding or a fee-shifting statute running in Defendant's favor. **No fee-shifting statute runs in Defendant's favor here.** § 604.18 is one-way to the insured; the MCFA is one-way to the consumer; the policy itself, on a careful read of `2014_newBusiness_policy.pdf`, contains no clause shifting attorney fees to the insured for first-party property disputes.

#### 2.5.2 The recoverable expert fees are capped at the statutory "reasonable" amount

Minnesota courts limit recoverable expert disbursements to amounts actually and reasonably paid for the expert's testimony — not for preparation, consultation, or report-writing. Defendant's actual expert spend on a first-party hail case can run $30,000 to $80,000, but only the portion attributable to compensable testimony at deposition and trial is recoverable as a disbursement. The recoverable portion typically caps at $5,000 to $15,000 even where Defendant's actual expert spend is larger.

### 2.6 Strategic implications

Three concrete takeaways for the family:

**First — the meaningful Defendant-cost risk in this case is approximately $10,000 to $30,000 in a worst-case scenario** (full defense win plus sanctions on weaker counts). That is real money but it is not the same scale as Plaintiffs' own out-of-pocket cost. The family's primary financial risk is its own legal expenses, not Defendant's.

**Second — the cleanest way to limit Defendant cost-recovery is to plead carefully and to drop weak counts at the first opportunity.** If A4 (in v3) draws a Rule 12 motion, voluntary dismissal under Rule 41(a) before opposition is filed eliminates the sanctions risk. If C3 falters at summary judgment on the "in connection with the sale" question, the family can voluntarily dismiss to avoid a frivolous-litigation finding on the disposition. v2 already implements this strategy by omitting A4 and B4 standalone.

**Third — Defendant's Rule 68 offer is the more important cost-recovery vector to track.** Once Defendant serves a Rule 68 offer (likely anchored at or just above the appraisal award figure), the family must respond strategically. If the offer is rejected and the eventual recovery is less than the offer, post-offer disbursements shift to Plaintiffs — a smaller amount than full attorney fees, but a category that grows with case duration. The family should plan for a counter-offer regime that locks in a defensible position, both for the § 549.09 prejudgment-interest mechanism and for the Rule 68 cost-allocation mechanism.

### 3.2 The single most important financial decision

The choice between hourly and contingent representation drives the family's near-term cash exposure more than any other variable. Hourly representation requires the family to fund the case as it proceeds — a six-figure cash commitment on a multi-year horizon. Contingent representation transfers most of that funding obligation to counsel — but is unavailable without counsel willing to take the risk, which the June 11, 2025 MN Lawyer Referral Service refund indicates may be hard to find.

The realistic scenarios:

- **Best case:** Contingent counsel found; family advances some costs (perhaps $10,000–$25,000); fee-shifting at the end reimburses most of it; recovery of $100,000–$300,000 net of contingent fee.
- **Middle case:** Hourly counsel retained; family pays $60,000–$120,000 in fees and costs through trial; fee-shifting at the end recovers $50,000–$100,000 of that; recovery of $50,000–$150,000 net.
- **Worst case:** Hourly counsel retained; family pays $90,000–$295,000 through trial; loses on the merits or below the deductible; no fee-shifting; potentially $5,000–$30,000 in Defendant taxable costs and sanctions on top.

### 3.3 Mitigations that meaningfully change the calculation

1. **Issue a Minn. Stat. § 549.09 Subd. 1(b) offer of settlement contemporaneously with filing.** Locks in the family's accrual posture and anchors the offer-of-settlement battleground favorably. *(See `D2.md` and `bowl.md` § 0.)*
2. **File v2 rather than v3.** Eliminates the meaningful sanctions exposure A4 creates without sacrificing settlement leverage.
3. **Drop weak counts voluntarily before they draw motion practice.** Rule 41(a) voluntary dismissal before opposition is filed eliminates sanctions exposure for that count.
4. **Pursue MCFA carefully.** The C3 count's "in connection with the sale" question is the most important pleading-quality decision in v2. Anchor the count in the *Force* continuing-relationship reading, plead both Route 1 (§ 8.31) and Route 2 (§ 325F.70), and keep MCFA damages distinct from contract damages.
5. **Develop the discovery record first; file the § 604.18 Subd. 4(a) motion only when the record supports prima facie deliberate disregard.** A premature Subd. 4(a) motion that fails wastes attorney time the family pays for and weakens the eventual motion when the record ripens.

### 3.4 Bottom line for the family

The litigation is filable and has reasonable settlement leverage, but it is not financially low-risk. The family should plan for **at least $25,000–$50,000 in front-loaded out-of-pocket costs** before any fee-shifting reimbursement, and should treat the maximum exposure as **$300,000+** in a worst-case-loss scenario through trial.

Defendant's recoverable taxable costs are a smaller but real component of that exposure — **typically $8,000–$15,000** in a defendant-prevails scenario, with sanctions exposure adding another **$0–$30,000** depending on which complaint draft is filed and how disciplined the family is about voluntary dismissal of weak counts.

The recommendation in `00_Overview_Complaint_Drafts.md` to file **v2 Balanced** with disciplined post-pleading motion practice (drop weak counts early, time the § 604.18 motion to discovery, develop the MCFA count carefully) is the posture that minimizes the family's risk-adjusted exposure while preserving the upside.
